    Strategic Marketing Management · Session 7

    Products, Brands and Brand Strategy

    Customers buy benefits, and most brands deliver about the same core benefit. This session shows where brands can still differ, why people pay more for them, who owns them, and when a brand name can safely move into new products.

    Chapter map

    Benefit customers buy→How they buy it→Brand meaning and assets→Who owns the brand→How the brand grows

    Key idea

    The lecturer applies one test to every product, rename and extension: what benefit does it offer, and why would this customer buy it? If that has no clear answer, the launch is unlikely to last.

    Based on the Session 7 slides and lecture, with questions from the lecturer’s discussion video added to Cold call practice.

    1. People buy benefits: three product levels

    Key idea

    A product is anything offered to a market that might satisfy a want or need: goods, services, places, people or ideas. Nobody wants a hammer for its own sake. They want the picture on the wall.

    LevelDefinitionHammer example
    Core product 核心產品The basic benefit or the problem being solved. Any brand delivers it.Driving a nail to hang a picture
    Actual product 實際產品The brand-specific, tangible parts: brand name, design, quality, features, packaging.Brand, grip, weight, look
    Augmented product 延伸產品Optional extras built around the offer: warranty, delivery, installation, after-sale service.Lifetime guarantee, free video tutorials
    PairHow to tell them apart
    Core / actual productCore is the benefit the buyer wants. Actual is the branded product that delivers it.
    Augmented / expectedAugmented is an extra on top. Over time customers start to expect it, and it becomes a basic requirement.

    How it works

    Remove the brand name and most competing products give about the same core benefit. The lecturer calls this a “dirty secret” of marketing. Brands therefore compete through the actual and augmented layers and through what the brand means. Grab grew quickly because it solved a real pain point, getting a taxi at busy times, even though riders pay an extra fee.

    Class exampleCoreActualAugmented
    UniversityEducationCampus, instructors, library, brand name, diplomaGym, clubs, on-campus doctor, alumni networking
    Airline (Qantas slide)Fast, time-critical transportSchedules, seats, meals, safety record, booking systemFrequent flyer scheme, lounge club, holiday packages
    Credit cardPay later; arguably also convenience and safetyThe card and its brandPurchase insurance, points, reserved parking, restaurant discounts
    Bottled waterHydrationBrand and bottleMaybe none; maybe a website, QR code or money-back guarantee

    A diploma is tangible, so it belongs to the actual product. Where “actual” ends and “augmented” begins depends on what customers expect: a physical library or meals included in tuition may be expected at one school and an extra at another.

    2. Emotion is part of the product

    Key idea

    Products are bundles of benefits, and some of those benefits are emotional. People can form relationships with brands, so changing the actual product can damage the core benefit.

    Class example · Perfume

    Asked what a perfume maker works on first, most people guess the fragrance. The lecturer says companies usually start with the brand and fit the scent later. Names such as Obsession, Poison, Envy and Happy carry the emotion. In production, perfume sits in large drums; the fragile bottle is part of what people pay for.

    Class example · New Coke

    Pepsi ran blind taste tests in unmarked cups, and slightly more people preferred Pepsi. Coke changed its formula until it also won blind tests, then launched New Coke. Loyal drinkers stockpiled the old product and sent angry calls and letters, and the original returned within months. The tests measured taste correctly but missed the relationship.

    How it works

    In the brain-scan studies the lecturer describes, responses to Coke and Pepsi look similar when the brand is hidden and differ once people are told the brand. Memories and advertising shape the experience, not just the label.

    3. How people buy: four product classes

    ClassHow people buyMarketing implicationExample
    ConvenienceOften and quickly, with little thought; low price; little difference where boughtWide availability and mass advertisingGum, soap, chips, Coke
    ShoppingCompare quality, price and style; search for informationShowrooms, salespeople and sales supportTV, phone, clothes, furniture, car
    SpecialtySpecial effort for that one brand; usually no comparison and no substituteFew outlets with expert staffLamborghini, Bang & Olufsen, Dom Pérignon, Rolex
    UnsoughtBuyers do not know it or do not think of buying itHeavy advertising, demonstrations, personal sellingLife insurance, As Seen On TV gadgets, many new innovations

    Reasoning

    The class tells you where and how to sell. A shopping product usually needs a place to see, try and ask questions; a convenience product needs to be everywhere. The lecturer stresses these are general patterns. Some buyers are highly involved even with cheap items: they walk across a mall for a Coke from one shop, or buy plastic-wrapped magazines from Kinokuniya. Tesla sells cars online, and specialty shops can lose buyers who learn in store and then buy online.

    Class example

    Specialty does not require a high price. Office workers who insist on som tam and grilled chicken from Soi Polo treat a cheap meal as a destination. For an unsought product, the first job is awareness: nobody plans a shopping trip for life insurance, so it is sold through calls and visits. The discussion video uses Apple AirTags to ask how a new, unsought product gains awareness and interest.

    4. The “why buy?” test

    Key idea

    “It’s new” only drives the first purchase. A product needs a reason people will buy it again.

    Pepsi Blue

    The lecturer calls it a gimmick. Cola is brown for a reason, the roasted kola nut, and blue has no link to the taste. Likely buyers are existing Pepsi drinkers, so most sales are cannibalized 自家互搶.

    Fanta blueberry

    The blue color matches a real flavor, so it has a reason to exist and, in the lecturer’s view, a better chance.

    Class example

    Foaming shampoo from Organics, then Lux foam products, were fun to try at about twice the price but gave adults no real benefit. In the lecturer’s words, someone invested in the technology and then asked what else it could do. These launches failed. The discussion video asks the same question about tom yum tea, a brown-yogurt drink and Coca-Cola Energy.

    中文邏輯新奇只換到第一次試買；要有理由讓顧客買第二次。

    5. Brands and distinctive brand assets

    Key idea

    A brand is a name, sign, symbol or design that identifies a seller and separates it from others. The word comes from the branding iron used to mark cattle. The lecturer’s complaint: most companies pick a name and a logo and stop there.

    A distinctive brand asset 獨特品牌資產 is a non-name element that is both unique (consumers link it to the right brand, not a competitor) and famous (most consumers know it). Assets need consistent use and regular reinforcement.

    ElementUnique?Famous?Verdict
    Coca-Cola script, red-and-white wave, bottle shapeYesYesStrong asset; even a cropped piece of the script is recognizable
    IBM striped letters; Starbucks sirenYesYesStrong assets that are hard to copy
    McDonald’s yellow on its ownNo; many brands use yellowYesWeak alone
    A generic tiger or crocodileNoVariesShared symbol; Tiger Balm, Lacoste and a farm-tool maker all use animals
    Purple for a bankNo; SCB and AEON both use itYesCan cause confusion

    Class example

    The lecturer twice walked into AEON thinking it was his SCB branch. That shows the risk of a shared color and raises a bigger question: do people choose a bank by color, or by interest rates and their employer’s payroll choice? You cannot own a color, but you can use one consistently, as Tiffany does with its blue. Copycats such as Gookie beside Pocky, and a Melbourne restaurant named Ohina whose font reads like “China” while it sells Japanese food, show how names and fonts can confuse.

    Reasoning

    If you use a character, make it yours: a standard elephant, tiger or crocodile is shared, so give it features no one else has. The discussion video makes the business point with an iPad beside a Galaxy Tab: when products work in similar ways, the brand, logo and recognizable elements do the differentiating.

    6. Why customers pay more for brands

    Key idea

    A Muji bag and a Louis Vuitton duffel both carry things. The large price gap comes from what the brand adds. The lecturer names consistency as the most important part: whatever the quality level, customers need to know what they will get.

    ReasonWhat it does for the customerClass example
    Consistency and convenienceSets an expectation and shortens the shopping list, the consideration set 考慮組合Nobody tries every shampoo or tea
    Quality assuranceLowers the risk of a bad choiceLouis Vuitton craftsmanship and heritage
    Status and self-expressionShows money, taste or identity to others“It’s not just a watch, it’s a Rolex.”
    Emotional appealPleasure, reward, a sense of friendshipHäagen-Dazs as an affordable treat; Coca-Cola as a friend
    FamiliarityKnown brands feel safer and more likeableThe mere exposure effect

    How it works

    Mere exposure effect 單純曝光效應: repeated exposure tends to increase liking, as with a song that grows on you after several plays. In a wine study the lecturer describes, people enjoyed the same wine more when told it was more expensive. Brand and price cues change the experience itself.

    Brand identity and brand image

    Brand identity

    What the company sets out: personality, values, how the brand looks and speaks.

    Brand image

    What customers actually perceive. Identity drives image over time.

    Class example

    Staff change, owners retire and companies are sold, so written values keep the brand consistent and guide choices about sponsorships and partners. If Malee is “the fruit expert,” a mushroom product is hard to justify. Brand relationships also differ by category: Coca-Cola can feel like a friend, while dishwashing liquid is more of a work relationship.

    7. Positioning, repositioning and rebranding

    Key idea

    Positioning is a planned brand meaning, communicated consistently, that rivals cannot easily copy. The lecturer says positioning, more than segmentation, is really about branding.

    Reasoning

    Avoid over-positioning 定位過窄: tying the name to one category limits where the brand can go. Starbucks dropped “Coffee” from its logo and Apple dropped “Computer.” A Thai DVD-player ad promising “it will not skip” positions on a basic requirement; any player should do that, though the message may still reassure a first-time buyer.

    Same imageNew imageSame name
    No change
    RepositioningPrickly Heat tried plastic cans, new scents and young celebrities; the lecturer doubts it worked.
    New name
    RebrandingTelecomAsia became True; the lecturer says service stayed the same.
    BothUtaitip became Utip when it moved from medicine to cosmetic; Dunkin’ Donuts became Dunkin’ to stress coffee.

    Class example

    The lecturer thinks the Dunkin’ change was unwise: “Dunkin’ Donuts” had alliteration and a clear meaning, and coffee is already crowded. Siang Pure and Peppermint Field show another route: run two brands with two images, one traditional and one modern, instead of stretching one.

    8. Brand equity: a useful idea, a weak measure

    Key idea

    Brand equity 品牌權益 is the value a brand adds beyond the product, similar to goodwill in accounting. The slide cites Kapferer: companies can lose money yet still be valued highly for the brands they own.

    Class example

    The lecturer’s test: two identical landlines, 100 baht a month from TOT or 120 baht from True. Paying the extra 20 baht only for the name would be brand equity. BMW versus Toyota is different: the cars promise different engineering and quality, so that price gap is positioning. He says businesses usually sell for about 5–7 times annual earnings, and recalls Nestlé paying about 26 times for the KitKat business.

    Limit: the lecturer distrusts brand-equity scores sold by research firms. In his view there is still no reliable measure, and a score does not tell a manager what to change.

    9. Naming a brand

    Key idea

    A good name suggests benefits, is easy to pronounce, is distinctive, translates well, and can be registered and protected. Check other countries early: Mama and Oishi could not use their names in some markets because the names were already taken.

    Optional · Sound symbolism

    The lecturer shares this for interest and says it is not part of the class. Across many languages, most people call a round shape “bouba” and a spiky one “kiki”; the maluma and taketa studies date from 1929. Long, deep vowels sound bigger and slower, so “Bromley” suits an SUV and “Brimley” a small sports car. Short names, such as the Nissan March, suit small cars.

    10. Who owns the brand: four sponsorship options

    OptionOwner and makerClass exampleMain risk
    Manufacturer’s brandThe producer owns it, and may have bought itIBM; P&G bought Pantene; PepsiCo bought GatoradeRetailers control shelf access
    Private label 自有品牌The retailer owns it; another firm makes itTops, 7-Eleven, Big C’s Leader PriceQuality can shift when the maker changes
    Licensed brandAnother party owns the name or characterHello Kitty, Snoopy, Disney characters; a German throat medicine made in Thailand under license; Ferrari laptopsThe owner can take the market back
    Co-brandingTwo firms make the product togetherGoPro and Red Bull; Oreo in a Dairy Queen Blizzard; MAC and Hello KittyThe partner must fit the positioning

    Class example

    If Starbucks made ice cream, a premium partner such as Häagen-Dazs would fit its positioning better than a cheap brand. In practice, the lecturer says, Starbucks has another firm make it and puts its own name on it. Consumers usually cannot tell whether a product is licensed, co-branded or contract-made.

    Reasoning

    MAC with Hello Kitty looks odd, a premium cosmetic beside a childhood character, but the lecturer sees a good match: it targets adults who grew up with Hello Kitty and can now afford MAC. Licensed luxury names on everyday items, such as an Armani tennis racket or a Ferrari laptop, sell image rather than performance.

    11. Private labels and retailer power

    Key idea

    Manufacturer brands used to be king. Retailers now earn higher margins from their own labels, charge fees for shelf space and can copy what sells.

    Pitch to the retailer’s buyer→Pay a slotting fee 上架費 to each retailer→Pay more for better shelf space→Removed if sales are weak→Possibly copied by a private label

    The lecturer estimates one-time slotting fees of 300,000–500,000 baht per retailer and removal after about four to six months of weak sales. He says private labels usually sell 20–30% cheaper.

    Class example

    Big C’s Leader Price shampoo copied Sunsilk’s bottle shape, and some shoppers bought it by mistake. Sunsilk faced a dilemma: change its own pack to move away (costly, but it leaves the copy looking dated) or keep it. Tesco Lotus’s cola resembled Coca-Cola and was made by a low-share producer with spare capacity; when the producer changes, the product may not stay consistent.

    Reasoning

    A discussion-video slide adds that Thailand was among the five fastest-growing private-label markets in 2000, yet private labels held only about 1.5% share in 2019, compared with 40–50% in leading markets. The retailer’s power comes more from the shelf, the fees and the sales data than from private-label share.

    12. Growing the brand: extensions, complements and line stretching

    MoveDefinitionClass exampleMain risk
    Line extension 產品線延伸Same brand, same category; new flavor, size or formSunsilk colors, Lay’s flavors, Oishi teasLittle difference; cannibalization
    Brand extension 品牌延伸Same name moved into a new categoryColgate toothbrushes and mouthwashPoor fit; Colgate’s frozen food failed
    Complementary productA new category built on the same core benefitArm & Hammer: deodorizing across laundry, cat litter and toothpasteWeak when the shared benefit is missing
    Stretching downA cheaper item under a premium nameMercedes A-Class, BMW 1 SeriesDilution 品牌稀釋
    Stretching upA premium item from a value brandToyota created Lexus as a separate nameLow credibility under the old name
    Line fillingMore items inside the current rangeMore than 30 Tylenol typesChoice confusion; cannibalization

    Key idea

    Most new launches are line extensions, and most fail: they are barely different, face heavy competition and take sales from the original, which usually remains the best seller. Companies still do it because it is fast, uses spare capacity and fills shelf space that rivals would otherwise take.

    Class example

    Extensions work along a shared benefit. Mr. Clean moved from household cleaners to car wash and tire shine, and Harley-Davidson sells clothing to people who may never own the bike. Colgate is so tied to oral hygiene that its frozen food failed, and St. Luke’s, known for prickly heat and skin care, struggles with toothpaste. The lecturer’s test question: would people fly Coke Airlines?

    Reasoning

    Stretching down is easier than stretching up: Montblanc could launch a lighter line more easily than a budget pen brand could compete with Montblanc. The lecturer would keep McCafé separate from McDonald’s; in Hong Kong, customers ordered the cheap McDonald’s coffee and sat on the McCafé side. MK Gold opened at Siam Paragon as a special premium version, then lost that feeling as more branches opened, while diners still mostly cook vegetables.

    14. Cold call practice

    Why buy a hammer?

    To get the benefit, such as a picture on the wall. People buy what a product does for them, and most brands deliver that same core benefit.

    What are the core, actual and augmented products of a car?

    Core: getting from place to place. Actual: brand, design, engine, features and quality. Augmented: warranty, free servicing, financing and roadside assistance.

    Are convenience products risky purchases?

    Usually not. They are cheap and bought often, so involvement is low, although some buyers are particular about where they buy even a cheap item.

    How does product class affect selling in a shop or online?

    Shopping and specialty products often need places to see, try and ask experts; convenience products need wide availability. Exceptions exist, such as Tesla selling online.

    Why is Pepsi Blue a gimmick?

    Its only reason to buy is novelty, which lasts one purchase. The color has no link to taste, and most buyers would have bought Pepsi anyway.

    What makes an element a distinctive brand asset?

    It is unique to the brand and famous among consumers, and it is used consistently. Shared colors and generic animals fail the uniqueness test.

    Why are people willing to pay more for branded products?

    Brands offer consistency, quality assurance and lower risk, plus status, self-expression and emotional value. Familiarity also increases liking.

    What is the difference between rebranding and repositioning?

    Rebranding changes the name; repositioning changes the image. TelecomAsia became True with little change in service; Prickly Heat tried to look younger without renaming.

    What is brand equity in the landline example?

    If two landlines are identical and customers pay 120 baht to True instead of 100 baht to TOT, the extra 20 baht paid for the name is brand equity.

    Why do retailers create private labels?

    They can earn higher margins and offer something other stores do not have. Their control of the shelf, fees and sales data also gives them bargaining power.

    What is the difference between a line extension and a brand extension?

    A line extension stays in the same category with a new flavor or size; a brand extension moves the name into a new category.

    Why do most line extensions fail?

    They are barely different, face heavy competition and take sales from the original product, which usually stays the best seller.

    15. Answer templates

    Explain the product levels

    At the core, customers buy [benefit], which any brand can provide. The actual product is where [brand] differs, through [brand name, design or packaging]. It could add [extra] as an augmented benefit, though that layer is optional.

    Evaluate a launch or extension

    This is a [line or brand] extension. It can [save advertising, use capacity or take shelf space], but the main risk is [cannibalization, poor fit or dilution]. The key test is whether customers see a clear benefit that fits what the brand already means.

    Rebrand or reposition

    The problem is [the name or the image]. I would [rebrand, reposition or both] because [reason]. A new name alone will not help if the customer experience stays the same, so I would check meaning, slang and registration in each market first.
