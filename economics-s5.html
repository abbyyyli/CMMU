    Monopoly and Price Discrimination

    A monopolist can influence price, but demand still limits sales. Choose output using marginal revenue and marginal cost, read price from demand, and use average total cost to check profit. Then ask who gains when prices differ across customers.

    Control over entry creates pricing discretion, not unlimited willingness to pay.

    Class opening: Greene Planet

    In Quantum of Solace, Dominic Greene's company seeks control of Bolivia's water supply. Exclusive access to an essential resource makes rival supply and substitution harder. The mechanism is control of an input.

    Medicine and legal exclusion

    The historical Combivir example links patent protection to restricted entry and pricing power. Scale economies, exclusive licenses, and temporary technological advantages are other possible barriers.

    The medicine example uses historical prices and patent conditions.

    A price maker selects a feasible point on demand. Raising price generally reduces quantity demanded; a higher price can therefore reduce total revenue or profit. Define the market and available substitutes before judging how much pricing power a firm has.

    2. Why marginal revenue is below price

    The key is how an extra sale changes revenue across the whole sales plan.

    Demand D shows the highest common price buyers will pay for each quantity. Marginal revenue MR shows how much total revenue increases when the seller expands output. They answer different questions.

    Under uniform pricing, a larger quantity requires a lower price on all units in that sales plan. The extra unit brings revenue in, while the lower price reduces receipts on the other units. As a result, MR lies below D. The relevant comparison is the extra revenue after this price effect, not simply the extra unit's selling price.

    Competitive firm

    Takes market price as given. An extra unit does not require a price cut on the other units, so MR=P.

    Single-price monopolist

    Faces downward-sloping demand. Expanding sales lowers the common price, so MR<P.

    If the next unit adds more revenue than cost, expansion improves profit. If it adds less, reducing output improves profit. The usual MR–MC crossing identifies where that incentive changes.

    Optional: linear demand and the numerical example

    Linear demand is a straight-line relationship: each additional unit requires the same price reduction. For linear inverse demand, MR has the same vertical intercept and twice the negative slope. This shortcut is specific to linear demand.

    TR = P × Q; MR = ΔTR / ΔQ
    P = a − bQ; continuous MR = a − 2bQ
    D reaches zero at Q = a/b; MR reaches zero at Q = a/(2b)

    In MRU's example, two units at $16 earn $32. Three units at $14 earn $42. The extra unit adds $10: its $14 receipt minus the $4 lost on the other two units. For whole-unit tables, use the actual change in revenue rather than a continuous derivative.

    How to build this graph
    1. Identify the question. A single-price monopolist chooses along downward-sloping demand. Use Q on the horizontal axis and price/cost per unit on the vertical axis.
    2. Start with D, then add MR. D gives buyers' price constraint. MR lies below it because expanding sales lowers the common price.
    3. Add MC and find output. Locate the relevant MR–MC crossing and project down to Qm. For whole-unit tables, find where an additional unit stops improving profit; exact equality is not required.
    4. Move up to D for price. At Qm, mark the point on D and read Pm. The height of the MR–MC crossing is not the selling price.
    5. Add only what the question needs. For profit or loss, add ATC and compare it with Pm at Qm. For efficiency, find D–MC and mark Qe; excluded beneficial trades lie between D and MC.

    Class drug graph: mark MR–MC at (80, 0.50), D at (80, 12.50), and ATC at (80, 2.50). These points share the same output but play different roles.

    3. The professor's whole-gallon decision

    Compare the benefit and cost of the next unit, rather than chasing the largest revenue.

    The class example makes the marginal decision concrete. The fourth gallon still increases profit; the fifth reduces it. The best listed choice is therefore four gallons per day at $7, with $11/day profit.

    Revenue keeps rising beyond the best output, but additional production also costs resources. More sales need not mean more profit. When quantities come in whole units, the decision can switch from a profitable extra unit to an unprofitable one without any row having exactly equal MR and MC.

    At the chosen output, price lies above ATC, so the firm earns a profit. The vertical gap between P and ATC is profit per gallon; the rectangle's width is the number of gallons. Fixed cost is paid even with zero production in this short-run example, so shutdown can be worse than operating.

    5. Elasticity, markup, and cost changes

    Less price sensitivity can support a larger markup; a steeper line alone is not enough.

    Elasticity需求彈性 measures percentage quantity response to percentage price change. Fewer close substitutes can make buyers less responsive. MRU's medicine discussion adds two mechanisms: serious illness can increase the priority of treatment, and third-party payment can reduce the user's sensitivity to the bill.

    The intuition: when buyers can easily switch, a price increase loses many sales and the seller's markup is constrained. With weaker substitutes, the seller can sustain a larger gap between price and MC. In the class comparison, “relatively inelastic” means less responsive than the other demand case.

    The standard positive-MC interior optimum remains on the elastic part of demand. On an inelastic part, reducing output and raising price would increase revenue while reducing variable cost, so the seller would want to change its choice.

    Optional: markup measures and the linear examples
    Absolute markup = P − MC
    Lerner index = (P − MC) / P = 1 / |ε|

    The Lerner formula applies at a differentiable, unconstrained interior single-price optimum; with positive MC, |ε|>1. The comparison uses P=60−2Q and P=140−4Q, both with MC=20. Their optimal markups are 20 and 60, and elasticity magnitudes are 2 and 4/3. Both the intercept and slope change; steepness alone does not identify elasticity.

    MRU's historical airline puzzle contrasts a Washington–Dallas ticket with a longer Washington–San Francisco journey through Dallas. Fewer convenient alternatives for the hub destination can support a larger markup. Distance alone does not determine fares.

    ChangeCurve logicResult, conditional on operating

    Higher unavoidable fixed costATC rises; MC stays fixed.Same optimal Q and P, lower profit.

    A monopolist generally has no demand-independent supply curve. Output depends on demand/MR as well as costs; MC is not a competitive supply schedule that can be used without demand.

    6. Profit, lost trades, and innovation

    A transfer to the seller differs from a loss of combined gains.

    ObjectMeaningGraph / calculation
    Consumer surplus消費者剩餘Willingness to pay minus payment.Area below D and above price for units sold.
    Producer surplusTR minus variable cost.Revenue minus the area under MC.
    Economic profit經濟利潤TR minus all economic costs.PS minus fixed cost in this model.
    Deadweight loss無謂損失Combined gains from trade that are lost.Area between D and MC over excluded beneficial units.

    Uniform monopoly pricing excludes some units whose WTP exceeds MC but falls below the monopoly price. Efficient output satisfies D=MC; monopoly output satisfies MR=MC. Lost consumer surplus includes both transfers and DWL.

    The monopoly price transfers some surplus from buyers to the seller. It also prevents trades that would create value because the buyer's willingness to pay exceeds the resource cost. The transfer changes who receives the gains; the missing trades reduce total gains. That is why all lost consumer surplus cannot be labeled DWL.

    Higher fixed cost can turn profit into a loss without changing the marginal output choice. If that fixed cost is unavoidable now, operating may still lose less than shutting down. Conversely, pricing at MC can serve efficient output while leaving fixed cost uncovered; efficient allocation and financing are separate questions.

    Optional: follow the MRU numerical example

    With P=100−2Q, MC=20 and FC=100: Qm=20, Pm=60, ATC=25, profit=700, CS=400, PS=800 and DWL=400. Efficient Q=40 raises CS+PS from 1,200 to 1,600 before common fixed cost. Consumers lose 1,200: 800 transfers to the seller and 400 disappears.

    At FC=1,000, output and price stay unchanged, but ATC becomes 70 and profit becomes −200. Operating still beats losing the unavoidable 1,000 through shutdown.

    Static allocation

    Use existing knowledge and resources efficiently. The diagram measures lost current trades.

    Dynamic incentives

    Expected patent profits may encourage innovation. Whether extra innovation outweighs access costs requires evidence.

    Patent buyouts and innovation prizes attempt to reward discovery separately from a high per-unit price. Their evaluation also requires financing, administration, and incentive effects. A possible innovation benefit does not validate every protected monopoly.

    MRU Office Hours example. Surplus excludes fixed cost; economic profit deducts it.

    7. Different prices, different customers

    Identify the information used, the resale constraint, and the relevant counterfactual.

    Price discrimination差別取價 charges different prices for comparable units for reasons beyond different supply costs. Sustaining it requires pricing power, a way to segment customers or induce sorting, and limits on arbitrage套利轉售.

    DeviceInformation / selectionExample
    First-degree / perfectExact unit-level willingness to pay願付價格.Charge each buyer's reservation value; sell while WTP≥MC.
    Second-degreeA menu induces self-selection.Different usage plans or restricted/flexible tickets.
    Third-degreeIdentify observable groups.Student, senior, or other verified customer categories.

    The class examples show senior and student Rabbit cards, and an admission sign charging Thai visitors ฿10 and foreigners ฿50. The cards illustrate observable categories; the sign shows a ฿40 gap and a fivefold price ratio.

    Class examples, slide 15. Rabbit fare amounts are not shown. The photographed admission prices are historical; the price gap alone does not identify demand elasticity.

    Resale can defeat segmentation: full-price buyers obtain discounted units through others. ID checks or nontransferable admission can help sustain separate prices. If resale forces a uniform price, the new optimum need not equal the former low price. Product differences also matter: a business-class fare gap alone does not isolate discrimination.

    Separate pricing lets the seller respond to each group's demand instead of compromising on one common price. The optimal group choices equate each group's MR with common MC. The group with less elastic demand at its chosen point supports a larger markup.

    Higher seller profit does not settle welfare. In this example, total output stays unchanged, but sales shift away from higher-value marginal buyers in A toward lower-value buyers in B. The seller gains while combined gains fall. Who receives the units matters alongside how many are sold.

    Under perfect discrimination, the seller can charge each unit's WTP without lowering prices on the other units. It expands until WTP meets MC, serving efficient output and capturing the gains. Consumer surplus can fall to zero even as DWL disappears; efficiency does not imply that every buyer benefits.

    8. Tying and bundling solve different problems

    Usage metering differs from a fixed package; revenue differs from profit.

    Tying and metering搭售與計量

    A base good is linked to repeated purchases of a tied good, such as a printer and required proprietary ink. A low access price can attract light users; recurring marked-up purchases collect more from heavy users.

    Bundling組合銷售

    Two or more goods are sold as a package. Pure bundling offers only the package; mixed bundling also offers components separately. The fixed package does not charge each time an app is used.

    Tying can increase system adoption while an above-MC consumable price reduces use per adopter. Both margins matter for welfare. Complementarity alone does not prove a tie; some restriction or practical dependency must connect purchases.

    A package can solve a pricing problem: one buyer values the first component highly, while another values the second highly. Separate high prices lose some useful sales. If their combined values are more similar, one package price can appeal to both buyers and increase consumption.

    At zero MC, separate Word=$100 and Excel=$90 earn $190. A $120 bundle sells to both and earns $240, an increase of $50, or about 26.3%. CS rises from zero to $10; total surplus rises from 190 to 250 because additional valuable components are consumed at zero incremental cost.

    Opposite component preferences make the buyers' bundle values more similar. Negative correlation helps but is not necessary; low incremental cost and sufficiently concentrated package values matter. With positive MC, unwanted components can consume real resources. Higher revenue alone does not establish higher economic profit or welfare.

    10. Cold call practice

    What makes a market a monopoly?

    One seller faces no close substitutes in a defined product and geographic market; entry barriers sustain its position.

    What is marginal revenue?

    The change in total revenue from an additional unit. Under uniform monopoly pricing, it includes revenue lost by repricing earlier units.

    What is economic profit?

    Total revenue minus all explicit and implicit economic costs, equal to (P−ATC)Q under a common price.

    Why can a price maker not choose any price and quantity?

    Demand links the two. A higher common price generally means fewer sales, so the firm must choose a feasible point on demand.

    What is deadweight loss?

    Lost total gains from trade. In the monopoly diagram, it lies between demand and MC over beneficial units not sold.

    What sustains group price discrimination?

    Pricing power, workable segmentation or sorting, and limits on resale. Separate group demand and costs determine the optimal prices.

    How does perfect discrimination change revenue?

    An extra unit can be sold at its own willingness to pay without reducing prior unit prices, so the seller expands while WTP covers MC.

    How do static and dynamic efficiency differ?

    Static efficiency concerns current allocation; dynamic efficiency concerns future innovation and improvements. A current price diagram does not measure the latter.

    11. Answer templates

    Price and profit

    “I ask whether another unit adds more revenue than cost. I identify the output where that incentive changes, then use demand to read its price. I compare price with ATC to explain profit or loss. For whole-unit choices, an exact MR–MC equality is unnecessary.”

    A change in costs

    “I identify whether marginal or fixed cost changed. A marginal-cost increase changes the MR–MC output choice and the price read from D. An unavoidable fixed-cost increase lowers profit without changing that interior choice, conditional on operating.”

    Pricing and welfare

    “I specify the feasible alternative, then compare quantities by buyer, willingness to pay, resource costs, CS, and PS. I deduct fixed and implementation costs where relevant. Greater seller profit alone does not settle welfare; innovation effects require separate evidence.”
