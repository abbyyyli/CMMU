    The Financial System

    Some people have money they do not need yet; others have plans they cannot pay for yet. This chapter follows the bridges between them: the interest rate that prices the crossing, the banks and markets that carry the money, why the bridges collapsed in 2008, and what all of it means for your own savings.

    1. Opening: two ways to judge an action

    The session opened by returning to moral economics, with Alvin Roth's Moral Economics on the first slide.

    LensDefinitionExample from the slidesDo not confuse with
    Deontology義務論"Deon" means duty: an act is right or wrong because of the rule or duty it follows, whatever the outcome.Kneeling during the anthem is wrong, because patriotism is a duty and disrespecting the flag breaks it.Following the law: a duty can be moral even where no law requires it.
    Consequentialism結果論An act is judged by its consequences; utilitarianism, the best-known version, adds up the gains and harms to everyone.Kneeling hurts nobody and raises awareness for oppressed minorities, so it is good.Self-interest: the consequences that count are everyone's, not only your own.

    The footbridge version of the trolley problem asks "focus on the consequences?" Pushing one person off the bridge would stop the trolley and save five. A strict consequentialist counts five against one; most people still refuse to push, which is a deontological intuition at work. Colin Kaepernick kneeling during the national anthem gets opposite verdicts from the two lenses, as the table shows.

    Airbnb: a market experiment

    Edelman, Luca and Svirsky (2016) sent booking requests from guest profiles that were identical except for names that signal race. Hosts said yes more often to guests with white-sounding names (about 1,150 against 940) and said no more often to guests with African-American-sounding names (about 870 against 660).

    Reading it with both lenses

    A deontologist says refusing a guest because of race is wrong whatever it costs or earns. A consequentialist asks about the harm to excluded guests and the gains and losses to hosts and the platform. Both lenses can condemn the same act for different reasons.

    Counts are read from Figure 2 of the paper as shown on the slide, and are approximate.

    2. Why people save and borrow

    Saving and borrowing move spending across time. Income is hump-shaped over a life; spending does not have to be.

    StageIncome vs consumptionActionExample
    YoungIncome below consumptionBorrowA loan for CMMU tuition.
    Prime working yearsIncome above consumptionRepay debt, saveContributions to a provident fund.
    RetiredIncome below consumptionDissave: spend past savingsLiving on the fund after 60.

    This is the life-cycle theory of saving. Smoothing consumption this way raises well-being even though lifetime income is unchanged. Three more forces shape how much people save. Time preference: some people strongly prefer spending now, others are patient. Nudges: when an employer enrolls workers in a retirement plan by default, they save more, although nothing about the incentives changed. Big investments: students borrow for education, firms borrow for projects, and entrepreneurs such as Howard Schultz, building Starbucks, raise money they do not have.

    3. The market for loanable funds

    Treat saving and borrowing like any other market. The good being traded is the use of money for a while, and the interest rate is its price.

    PartDefinitionExampleDo not confuse with

    Demand to borrowSlopes down: a lower interest rate makes more projects worth borrowing for.A Thai SME buys a machine when loan rates fall.Demand for money to hold as cash.

    1. Quantity of loanable funds on the horizontal axis, the interest rate on the vertical axis. Draw S (savings) sloping up and D (borrowing) sloping down; mark E1, r1, Q1.

    There is no single building called the market for loanable funds. There are many markets for different borrowers and lenders, linked by banks, bond markets and stock markets. The model shows the raw forces behind interest rates. The rate that matters for decisions is the real interest rate實質利率: if a Thai bank pays 2% and prices rise 2%, a saver's buying power does not grow at all.

    4. What banks do

    The slides draw three bridges from lenders and savers to borrowers: banks, bond markets and stock markets. These are financial intermediaries金融中介.

    Pay interest

    Deposits earn interest, which attracts savings.

    Screen borrowers

    Loan officers do the due diligence that no single saver could afford.

    Pool and spread risk

    Many small deposits fund many loans, so a few bad loans hurt no one badly.

    Keep reserves

    Only a fraction of deposits is kept as cash for withdrawals; the rest is lent.

    The bank earns the spread: it might pay depositors 2% and lend at 7%. MRU builds the case with Starbucks.

    ArrangementProblem
    You alone lend Howard Schultz $1 million.If the venture fails, the loss to you is huge.
    You and 99 friends lend $10,000 each.Risk is shared, but 100 people each studying the business plan is slow and costly.
    Appoint one coffee-market expert to evaluate for everyone.This is what a bank does: specialists do the screening.
    A bank pools deposits.Now it is like 100,000 people lending Schultz $10 each, and lending to thousands of other entrepreneurs too. A few bad loans will not bankrupt the bank.

    The graph assumes borrowers default independently. In 2008 many mortgages failed together, which pooling cannot fix (section 9).

    Liquidity transformation流動性轉換

    Your deposit can be withdrawn any day, while the loan it funds is locked up for years.

    Maturity transformation期限轉換

    Short-term deposits fund long-term loans such as a 20-year mortgage.

    The same feature makes banks both useful and fragile. A bank counts on depositors not all asking for their money on the same day. If they do, even a sound bank runs out of cash, because the money is tied up in loans. The 2022 Nobel Prize in economics, shown in the slides, went to Ben Bernanke, Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig "for research on banks and financial crises". Diamond and Dybvig modeled exactly this: why banks that turn short deposits into long loans are valuable, and why that leaves them open to runs.

    中文邏輯銀行賺的是「資訊和分散風險」的錢；它把隨時要還的存款變成很久才收回的貸款，這是它最大的貢獻，也是最容易出事的地方。

    5. Stock markets

    A stock is a share of ownership with a claim on the firm's profits. Most stock trading does not give the firm a single baht.

    MarketDefinitionExampleDoes the firm get money?
    Primary marketThe firm sells new shares, first at its IPO首次公開發行, later in new issues.Starbucks went public in 1992; a Thai startup listing on the SET.Yes
    Secondary marketInvestors trade existing shares with each other.Everyday trading on the Stock Exchange of Thailand.No

    The secondary market still matters. Because shares are easy to resell, investors are willing to buy new issues in the first place. An IPO also rewards founders and venture capitalists who backed the firm when it was risky, and lets them diversify. Buying a stock is a bet on the company, so some shareholders end up happy and others "a little bit sad"; bank depositors usually do not see the value of their deposits swing.

    Same distinction as Session 7: buying existing shares is not investment in GDP, which counts only new capital goods.

    6. Bonds: IOUs, risk and yield

    A bond is an IOU: it says who owes how much, and when. A well-known firm like Starbucks, which has issued over $1 billion of bonds, can borrow directly from investors and skip the bank.

    BondStock
    You holdA loan to the firmPart ownership
    You receiveA fixed sum on a fixed date, sometimes with regular coupon paymentsA share of profits, as dividends or growth
    Who is paid firstBondholdersShareholders get what is left
    Main riskDefault risk: the borrower cannot payThe business does badly

    Riskier borrowers pay more. Rating agencies such as Standard & Poor's grade bonds from AAA down to D; anything below BBB- is called a junk bond. In the video Starbucks is rated A-, and Illinois (A-) pays more to borrow than Virginia (AAA). Collateral lowers the rate too: if you default on a mortgage the bank takes the house, but "once you've been to Maui, the bank can't repossess your vacation."

    A bond's yield殖利率 is the gain from holding it divided by the price paid. The face value is fixed, so the only way a bond can offer a higher yield is to sell for less.

    MRU's lesson from the three bonds: the 14.8% bond is not a free lunch. Equally risky assets must pay the same return, or everyone would buy the higher-yield one until its price rose. A high yield is a signal of high risk. Which bond to buy depends on how much risk you can live with.

    7. Crowding out: when the government borrows

    In 2016 the US government owed the public almost $14 trillion. A borrower that large moves the whole market.

    $ billionBeforeAfter the government borrows 100
    Interest rate7%9%
    Savings supplied200250
    Private borrowing200150
    Government borrowing0100

    Two things give way. Saving rises by 50, and to save more people consume less, so private consumption falls by 50. At 9% private borrowers want only 150, so private investment falls by 50. Together that is crowding out排擠效果: 100 of government borrowing displaces 50 of consumption and 50 of investment.

    The 50/50 split

    It comes from the slopes MRU drew. A steeper savings supply curve puts more of the burden on investment.

    Limit

    This is a full-employment, closed-economy result. Crowding out is smaller in a recession or when foreign savers fund the borrowing, and borrowing for productive infrastructure can raise future output.

    8. Four reasons the bridges fail

    Tyler Cowen compares intermediaries to bridges: we notice how much we rely on them only when they crumble. In many poor countries a working bridge never existed.

    ReasonWhat it destroysMechanismMRU examples
    1. Insecure property rightsWillingness to saveIf deposits or shares can be frozen or seized, people keep cash and gold instead.Cyprus seized deposits in 2013; Argentina and Brazil froze them; Russia and Yukos.
    2. Interest rate controlsThe price signalA usury law is a price ceiling on loans: below equilibrium it creates a shortage of credit.Cowen has seen loans at 50% or 100% and asks whether that is always bad.
    3. Politicized lendingScreening by meritLoans follow political connections; insolvent zombie banks are kept alive.Japan's lost decade, 1990 to 2000; more state-owned banks go with slower growth.
    4. Runs, panics, scandalsTrustWith fractional reserves, a rush to withdraw can sink even a sound bank: a bank run擠兌.Almost half of US banks failed in the Great Depression; Enron, WorldCom, Madoff.

    Use the "Interest rate cap" setting in the graph in section 7 to see reason 2: a cap at 5% raises the quantity demanded to 250, cuts the quantity supplied to 150, and actual lending falls from 200 to 150. The borrowers shut out are usually the riskiest, who then go without or turn to illegal lenders.

    After the Depression runs, the US created deposit insurance (the FDIC). If depositors know they will be repaid even when the bank fails, nobody needs to rush. The cost is moral hazard: insured depositors stop watching the bank, and banks may take more risk, which is why insurance comes with regulation.

    The link between state-owned banks and slower growth is a cross-country correlation; weak institutions may cause both.

    中文邏輯四個原因各破壞一樣東西：沒收存款破壞財產權，利率上限破壞價格，政治放款破壞篩選，擠兌讓信任瞬間崩潰。

    9. The Great Recession

    Most of the slides follow this story. The economy stands on a cliff edge with a load hanging below it, and MRU adds four weights one at a time. Then house prices fall, and the load drops.

    Weight 1: homeowner leverage

    A $100,000 house bought with a typical 20% down payment carries an $80,000 mortgage and $20,000 of owner's equity: what the owner would keep after selling and repaying the loan. Equity is the cushion. The leverage ratio槓桿比率 divides the mortgage by the owner's equity.

    Leverage ratio = mortgage ÷ owner's equity
    20% down: 80,000 ÷ 20,000 = 4
    5% down: 95,000 ÷ 5,000 = 19

    Before the crisis many homes were bought with 10%, 5% or even zero down. With 5% down, a fall to $90,000 leaves the house worth less than the $95,000 still owed: the owner is underwater, and equity is gone. That hurts the bank too, because in a foreclosure it gets a house worth less than the loan.

    Weight 2: bank leverage

    Banks did the same thing with their own balance sheets. Lehman Brothers' leverage ratio rose from about 20 in 2004 to 44 by 2007. At 44, a fall of about 2.2% in the value of its assets wipes out its equity; when the fall came, Lehman was underwater and went bankrupt on September 15, 2008. Why would experienced managers do this? Cowen gives two reasons: overconfidence that house prices could not fall much, and incentives, since bonuses rose with profits while the managers did not personally go bankrupt when the bank did.

    Weight 3: securitization

    Securitization證券化 bundles many individual mortgages into one tradable security and sells it to outside investors. The slides show four mortgages going into one package:

    MortgageHouse valueMortgageEquity
    1$81,000$77,000$4,000
    2$86,000$78,000$8,000
    3$85,000$75,000$10,000
    4$83,000$71,000$12,000

    The slide labels the third mortgage's equity $8,000; house value minus mortgage gives $10,000.

    Standard & Poor's, Moody's and Fitch rated such packages, often AA- or even AAA. Many turned out to be riskier than advertised, through outright fraud, rating failures, and above all complacency about house prices. Securitization itself is a useful tool: it frees cash for banks and spreads risk to investors who want it. The failure was in quality, transparency and ratings.

    Weight 4: the shadow banking system

    Part of the shadow banking systemNames on the slide
    Investment banksLehman Brothers, Bear Stearns
    Parts of commercial banksCitibank, Bank of America
    Money market fundsVanguard, PIMCO
    Issuers of asset-backed securitiesBNP Paribas
    Hedge fundsBridgewater, Man, Brevan Howard

    A commercial bank is funded by insured deposits. An investment bank such as Lehman is funded by investors, mostly through short-term loans, with no comparable guarantee: "use at your own risk." These lenders are watchful and quick to flee. Shadow banks borrow short to lend long like a bank, but without deposit insurance, and by 2008 they lent more than traditional commercial banks.

    The nudge: house prices fall

    2007: house prices fall→Homeowners underwater, defaults rise→Mortgage securities, even AAA, lose value→Leveraged banks near insolvency→Short-term lenders run from shadow banks→Fire sales push prices lower→Credit crunch, layoffs, store closings

    The last slide shows the response: a bank leverage ratio of 16.66, meaning more equity behind each dollar of assets. Cowen thinks requiring more equity and less leverage makes sense; it has been enacted, and "it remains to be seen" how well it works. A government guarantee for shadow banks would be the radical alternative, but it could expose taxpayers to trillions and would give leveraged managers even more reason to take risk.

    中文邏輯2008 不是單一原因，而是四層脆弱疊在一起：屋主槓桿、銀行槓桿、看不懂的房貸證券、沒有存款保險的影子銀行。房價一跌，短期資金一夜抽走，橋就斷了。

    10. The Thai bond market

    Before 1997 Thailand's financial system stood on one leg: bank loans. When banks and finance companies got into trouble, firms had nowhere else to borrow. After the crisis the country built a second bridge.

    Share of GDP1997Sep 2021
    Bank loans131%112%
    Bond market12%94%
    Corporate bonds3%27%
    Stock market capitalization24%117%

    In September 2021 the market held 14.9 trillion baht of bonds: government bonds including T-bills 45%, corporate bonds 27%, Bank of Thailand bonds 20%, state-enterprise bonds 7%, foreign bonds 1%. Government-related bonds are therefore about 72%. That shows who the big borrower is; on its own it does not prove crowding out, since corporate bonds grew over the same period.

    11. Your own money: four rules

    MRU's Money Skills videos turn the same economics into personal advice.

    RuleWhyEvidence MRU uses
    1. Ignore expert stock pickersPast winners rarely repeat, and luck is hard to tell from skill.Malkiel's dart-throwing monkey; of the top quarter of funds, under 4% stay on top two years later, 1% after five.
    2. It is hard to beat the marketThe efficient market hypothesis效率市場假說: prices already reflect public information."America is aging, buy nursing homes" is already priced in; after the 1986 Challenger explosion, Morton Thiokol fell over 11% that day, months before it was found responsible.
    3. Diversify, keep fees lowDiversification分散投資 lowers risk without lowering expected return.Enron employees held about 60% of their retirement savings in Enron stock and lost both job and savings.
    4. Even an imperfect market is more rational than youInvestors are overconfident and herd; anomalies exist but are small and fade once known.Shiller's Nobel work; Monday, January and momentum effects; Buffett tells his heirs to buy index funds.

    The coin-flip experiment shows why rule 1 holds. Give 1,000 "experts" a coin to predict whether the market rises. After one year 500 are right, after two 250, and after five about 31 have never been wrong (MRU says 32). They will be called geniuses and write books, yet they were certain to exist whatever the market did.

    MRU quotes about $57,000 and $70,000; those cannot be reproduced with a plain 8% return. The graph uses 10,000 × (1.08 − fee)^25, which gives $54,274 and $65,384. The lesson is the same.

    Diversification removes the risk specific to one firm, not the risk of the whole market falling, as it did in 2008. It also argues against home market bias: a Thai investor with her savings in her employer's shares and a condo in the same city has job, shares and property all exposed to one local shock.

    12. Rent or buy?

    An MRU Econ Duel: Tyler Cowen argues for buying, Alex Tabarrok for renting. They end closer than they start.

    IssueTyler: buyAlex: rent
    InvestmentDoes not dispute it.House prices are not guaranteed to rise; do not expect a great investment.
    TaxesMortgage interest is deductible, so buying can be cheaper.Much of the tax break is built into the price, so the seller captures it.
    ElasticityWhere builders can add homes (Texas, Florida), the price falls back and the buyer keeps the benefit.Where permits are nearly impossible (San Francisco, New York, Boston), landowners get it.
    StabilityA family wants the school district and no rent renegotiation.Only if you value stability more than the average buyer; owners pay for roofs and heating.
    DiversificationBetter for the wealthier.A house puts a big share of wealth in one asset in one place.
    Tax break for buyers→Housing demand shifts right→Fixed supply: price up a lot, seller gains/Elastic supply: more homes, buyer gains

    Where they agree: it is a marginal question. The price already reflects the average buyer's tax benefit, taste for stability and repair costs, so you gain from buying only where you differ from the average buyer. A low down payment is also high leverage (5% down is a ratio of 19), and one house is the opposite of diversification.

    The duel assumes US tax rules; Thai taxes, mortgage terms and condo markets differ.

    13. Do not mix these up

    Higher saving raises interest rates.

    More saving shifts supply right, so the interest rate falls.

    A 5% deposit rate makes me 5% richer.

    Subtract inflation: the real rate is what counts.

    The bank keeps my deposit in the vault.

    Most is lent out; only a fraction is held as reserves.

    Buying shares on the SET gives the company money.

    Only new issues do; trading existing shares pays the previous owner.

    Bondholders own part of the firm.

    They are lenders; shareholders are the owners.

    A high yield is a bargain.

    Equally risky assets pay the same; a high yield signals high risk.

    When interest rates rise, bond prices rise.

    They move in opposite directions.

    An interest rate cap makes loans cheaper for everyone.

    It creates a shortage; total lending falls and risky borrowers are shut out.

    Bank runs only hit bad banks.

    With fractional reserves, even a sound bank cannot pay everyone at once.

    Leverage ratio = debt ÷ assets.

    Here it is debt ÷ equity: 95,000 ÷ 5,000 = 19.

    2008 was a run on ordinary bank deposits.

    It was a run on the uninsured shadow banking system.

    Securitization is fraud.

    It is a legitimate tool; the problem was quality, opacity and ratings.

    A five-year winning record proves skill.

    About 31 of 1,000 coin flippers would have one.

    Diversification removes all risk.

    It removes firm-specific risk, not market-wide risk.

    The Thai bond market is mostly corporate bonds.

    Deontology and consequentialism always disagree.

    14. Cold call practice

    What is the difference between deontology and consequentialism?

    Deontology judges an act by the duty or rule it follows; consequentialism judges it by its outcomes. On kneeling during the anthem, one calls it a breach of patriotic duty, the other a harmless way to raise awareness.

    What is the market for loanable funds?

    The model where the supply of savings meets the demand to borrow, with the interest rate as the price, for example Thai household deposits meeting Thai firms' demand for loans.

    What happens when households save more?

    The supply of savings shifts right, so the interest rate falls and the quantity of lending rises.

    What does a bank do beyond holding your money?

    It pays interest, screens borrowers, pools many deposits into many loans so risk is spread, and keeps reserves for withdrawals. It earns the spread between loan and deposit rates.

    When does the stock market turn saving into investment?

    Only when new shares are issued and sold, as in an IPO. Trading existing shares only changes who owns them.

    A $1,000 one-year bond sells for $871. What is its yield, and what does it tell you?

    129 ÷ 871 = 14.8%. Equally risky assets pay the same return, so a yield this high signals high default risk.

    What is crowding out?

    Government borrowing raises the interest rate and displaces private spending. In MRU's example, 100 billion of borrowing raises the rate from 7% to 9% and cuts investment and consumption by 50 billion each.

    Name the four reasons financial intermediaries fail.

    Insecure property rights, interest rate controls, politicized lending, and runs, panics and scandals.

    Why can a bank run destroy a healthy bank?

    Banks hold only fractional reserves and lend the rest long term, so no bank can repay all depositors at once.

    What is the leverage ratio with 5% down on a $100,000 house?

    95,000 ÷ 5,000 = 19. A fall of just 5% in the house price wipes out the owner's equity.

    What is the shadow banking system, and why was it vulnerable?

    Investment banks, hedge funds, money market funds and other institutions that borrow short and lend long like banks, but without deposit insurance, so lenders run at the first sign of trouble.

    What is the efficient market hypothesis?

    Prices already reflect all public information, so you cannot systematically beat the market by trading on it. Morton Thiokol's shares fell within hours of the Challenger explosion.

    Why is your employer's stock a bad retirement investment?

    It concentrates wealth in one asset, and if the company fails you lose your job and your savings together, as Enron's employees did.

    How did Thailand's financial system change after 1997?

    The bond market grew from 12% to 94% of GDP while bank loans fell from 131% to 112%, giving firms a second way to borrow. About 72% of bonds are government-related.

    15. Answer templates

    Loanable funds

    “In the market for loanable funds, savers supply funds and borrowers demand them, and the interest rate is the price. If households save more, supply shifts right, the interest rate falls and lending rises. If firms become more optimistic or get an investment tax credit, demand shifts right and the interest rate rises.”

    What banks do

    “A bank pools many small deposits, screens borrowers on savers' behalf, and spreads risk across many loans, earning the spread between loan and deposit rates. Because it turns deposits that can be withdrawn any day into long-term loans, it is useful but open to runs.”

    Bonds and risk

    “A bond is an IOU with a fixed face value. Riskier issuers must offer higher yields, and since the face value is fixed, a higher yield means a lower price. Ratings and collateral lower the rate a borrower pays.”

    Crowding out

    “Government borrowing shifts the demand for loanable funds right and raises the interest rate. In MRU's example, 100 billion of borrowing raises the rate from 7 to 9 percent, cutting private investment by 50 billion and consumption by 50 billion.”

    The Great Recession

    “Four weights hung on the economy: homeowners with little equity, banks such as Lehman with leverage of 44, mortgage securities riskier than their ratings, and shadow banks funded by uninsured short-term loans. When house prices fell in 2007, lenders ran from the shadow banks, fire sales pushed prices lower, and the result was a credit crunch and high unemployment.”

    Your money

    “Prices already reflect public information and past winners rarely repeat, so do not pick stocks. Buy a diversified, low-fee index fund, including international stocks, and avoid holding your employer's stock.”
