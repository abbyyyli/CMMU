    Asymmetric Information

    When one side of a deal knows more than the other, two things can go wrong. Hidden quality or risk changes who shows up to trade, and hidden behavior changes what people do after a contract is signed. This session traces both problems, the fixes that reduce them, and what they mean for the value of a degree.

    1. Who knows what, and what is hidden?

    Start every case by naming the informed side. Then ask whether the hidden thing already exists or is a later choice.

    ConceptDefinitionExampleDo not confuse with
    Imperfect informationA decision maker lacks some relevant knowledge.Two traders are both unsure about next year's flood.Asymmetric information, which needs one side to know more.
    Asymmetric information資訊不對稱One party has better relevant information than the other.A seller knows the car was flood-damaged; the buyer cannot tell.Uncertainty that both sides share.
    Hidden typeAn existing characteristic the other side cannot observe, such as quality, health risk or ability.Engine damage that existed before the sale.A choice made after the contract.
    Hidden actionBehavior the other side cannot fully observe, such as effort or precaution.An owner skips agreed maintenance after buying coverage.A trait the person already had.

    “Before or after signing” is a handy shortcut, not the definition. Ask whether the problem is selection among existing types or behavior that responds to incentives. Plans can be made before signing, and private information can last after it.

    2. Adverse selection in used cars

    Buyers who cannot tell quality apart pay for the average car, and paying the average drives good cars away.

    In Akerlof's lemons model, sellers know whether a car is a reliable plum or an unreliable lemon劣質車, but buyers cannot tell. Buyers therefore pay for the expected quality on offer, and that price is too low for some owners of good cars.

    Quality hidden→Buyers pay for the average→Good cars withdrawn→Average quality falls→Offers fall again

    In this example the market shrinks to lemons only, but it does not disappear: lemons still trade between ฿80,000 and ฿100,000. Adverse selection逆向選擇 destroys the gains from good-car trades, not necessarily the whole market. Real used-car markets survive partly because inspections, vehicle histories, warranties and dealer reputation narrow the information gap.

    中文邏輯不是「便宜的車一定差」；是買家分不出品質，只能出平均價，好車車主因此退出，市場平均品質真的被拉低。

    Optional: the numbers behind the graph

    With equal numbers of each type, the pooled offer is 0.5×300 + 0.5×100 = 200. That is below the good-car minimum of 260, so good cars leave. Buyers then expect only lemons and offer 100, which lemon owners accept because 100 ≥ 80. With verified quality, both types would trade.

    3. Insurance pools and the death spiral

    In insurance the buyer often holds the private information, so the premium decides which risks stay in the pool.

    TermMeaningExample
    Expected lossThe probability-weighted average loss.A 10% chance of a ฿100,000 claim gives ฿10,000.
    Actuarially fair premiumA premium equal to expected covered claims, before admin costs and profit.Expected claims of ฿10,000 imply a fair premium of ฿10,000.
    Risk pooling風險分攤Combining many uncertain losses so premiums can pay the claims.Many households pay into one accident fund.
    Risk aversion風險趨避Preferring a certain payment to a risky one with the same expected value.Paying ฿15,000 to avoid a risk that averages ฿10,000.
    Death spiralHigher premiums push out lower risks, which raises premiums again.Each repricing leaves a riskier pool.

    A common premium follows the pool's average claims. If lower-risk buyers find it poor value and leave, the average and the premium rise. Risk aversion slows this: a careful buyer may pay above expected claims for peace of mind.

    Adverse selection

    Higher-risk people are keener to buy, so the insured pool is riskier than the population.

    Advantageous selection

    Cautious people may both prevent losses and buy coverage, so the insured pool can be safer than the uninsured. Which pattern holds is an empirical question for each product and price.

    Optional: MRU's stylized death spiral

    In MRU's teaching example, covered costs spread evenly from $0 to $30,000, so the first premium averages $15,000. If everyone expecting less than that drops out, the remaining pool averages $22,500, and the cycle can repeat. A continuous range of risks is what turns a smaller pool into a full spiral.

    4. Fixing adverse selection

    Either reveal the hidden type or change who joins the pool. Each fix has a cost.

    Screening篩選 is started by the less-informed side, such as a buyer ordering an inspection or an insurer asking for verifiable records. Certification passes on the result of a credible test; a badge with no real test behind it adds little.

    ResponseHow it helpsLimit
    Independent inspection or vehicle historyVerified quality lets prices match quality, so good sellers can return.Costs money, can miss damage, and the inspector may have conflicts.
    Seller warrantyLemons generate more claims, so the promise carries information (section 8).Works only if enforceable and costly for low types to copy.
    Risk classificationPremiums follow observed risk predictors, so low risks face fairer prices.High risks may face unaffordable terms, and less risk is shared.
    Group insuranceMembership is not chosen on individual health risk, which broadens the pool.Plan design can still attract some risks, and coverage may be tied to a job.
    Broad enrollment incentives or requirementsBringing in lower risks lowers average claims and steadies premiums.Results depend on take-up, subsidies, enforcement and benefit design.

    中文邏輯分得越清楚，越不會逆向選擇；但高風險的人也可能越難負擔。效率、風險分攤和公平是不同的問題。

    5. Moral hazard: coverage changes behavior

    Moral hazard is about incentives under imperfect monitoring, not about bad people.

    Moral hazard道德風險 arises when someone does not bear the full consequences of an action that others cannot fully observe. Insurance, guarantees, delegation and incomplete contracts can all shift consequences. No lie or fraud is needed.

    Shop buys fire insurance→Insurer bears much of a fire loss→Private payoff from prevention falls→Precautions slip→Expected claims rise

    This is not adverse selection: the same owner changes behavior after the contract, rather than a riskier type choosing to buy.

    Insurance and the use of care

    If a visit uses ฿1,000 of real resources but the patient pays a ฿200 copayment, the patient faces a lower price. That is a movement along the demand curve for visits, not a shift of it. The ฿1,000 cost does not disappear; the pool pays the difference.

    More insured care is not automatically waste. Some extra visits are worth less than their cost; others were blocked by cash or uncertainty and are worth more. Compare marginal benefit with resource cost before calling the change a loss.

    中文邏輯「道德」二字容易誤導。重點是行為的成本與利益分配變了，未必有人說謊。

    6. Principal-agent problems

    Hiring an expert helps because they know more, and that same gap creates room for conflict.

    A principal委託人 hires an agent to do a task. Problems arise when the agent's better information combines with incentives that differ from the principal's. MRU's examples are a mechanic who may recommend unneeded repairs, a taxi driver who knows the routes better than a tourist, and an employee whose effort is hard to see.

    ElementRepair case
    PrincipalCar owner who wants a reliable repair at a fair cost.
    AgentMechanic who diagnoses the problem and also sells the repair.
    Information gapOnly the mechanic can judge whether a part really needs replacing.
    ConflictExtra billed work pays the mechanic even when the owner does not need it.
    Wider lossFearing overcharges, owners refuse even useful preventive work.

    The deepest cost is not only overcharging. Distrust makes people turn down trades that would have helped them.

    Shirking is the effort version: when effort is costly and pay barely depends on it, an agent may do less than agreed. A bad outcome alone does not prove shirking, because luck, demand and tools also matter.

    7. Reducing moral hazard

    Narrow the information gap, or change who bears the consequences.

    ToolHow it worksLimit
    Monitoring and auditsBetter observation raises the chance of detection, so misconduct pays less.Costly, and people may game the measured number.
    Reputation and reviewsBad conduct today costs future customers, and reviews also inform buyers.Fake reviews and one-off sellers weaken it.
    Independent diagnosis獨立診斷The assessor cannot profit from selling the repair, so there is less reason to exaggerate.Independence does not guarantee competence.
    Warranty on the workPoor work leads to seller-paid repeat repairs.Must be clear and enforceable.
    Pay linked to resultsThe agent shares the gains from good outcomes.Outcomes are noisy, and metrics can be gamed.
    Cost sharingThe insured bears part of each loss or bill, so private incentives move closer to real cost.Weakens protection and may deter valuable care.

    Three kinds of cost sharing

    TermRuleSimplified example
    DeductibleThe insured pays losses up to a set amount before the insurer starts paying.A ฿12,000 claim with a ฿5,000 deductible: insured ฿5,000, insurer ฿7,000.
    CopaymentA fixed amount per covered service.฿200 per eligible visit.
    CoinsuranceA share of each eligible bill.10% of a ฿12,000 bill: insured ฿1,200, insurer ฿10,800.

    These are separate examples, not one combined policy.

    Information as a public good

    A trustworthy product test is costly to produce, but once shared it is nonrival and hard to keep from non-payers. Users free ride, so reliable testing may be underprovided. That raises the question of how independent information is financed; it does not mean every review platform fails.

    8. Signaling: making a claim believable

    A signal works when the low type would not want to copy it.

    Signaling訊號傳遞 is started by the informed side. In MRU's historical example, Hyundai improved its cars and backed them with an unusually long warranty. For reliable cars, expected warranty claims are low; for unreliable cars, the same promise would be expensive. That cost difference is what makes the warranty believable.

    QuestionSignalingScreening
    Who acts?The better-informed side.The less-informed side.
    Car exampleSeller offers a warranty.Buyer orders an inspection.
    Key testWould a low type want to copy it?Does the test actually separate the types?
    TermMeaning
    Separating outcomeDifferent types make different choices, so the choice reveals the type.
    Pooling outcomeTypes make the same choice, so uncertainty remains.
    Cheap talkA costless claim like “excellent condition” that any seller can make, so it separates nothing.

    A credible signal need not be costly for the good type. What matters is that copying is costly for the bad type and that the promise can be honored. A warranty also transfers risk and changes later incentives for repair quality and buyer care.

    9. Education: skills, signals or prior ability?

    Graduates earn more. Three mechanisms can explain it, and they can work at the same time.

    ExplanationWhat changesChainExample
    Human capital人力資本Productive skills.Study builds skills, output rises, and employers value the worker more.An analyst learns spreadsheet modeling and forecasts better.
    SignalingWhat employers know about existing traits.Finishing a hard degree shows persistence, employers update their view, and offers improve even with unchanged skills.A demanding degree helps an employer infer persistence.
    Ability biasWho gets educated.Prior ability or resources raise both schooling and earnings, so the raw gap overstates the effect of schooling.Highly motivated students might earn more even without the degree.

    中文邏輯薪水較高，可能是學到東西，可能是雇主終於看出能力，也可能本來就有優勢；同一筆相關資料分不出三者的比例。

    The sheepskin effect文憑完成溢價 is a jump in earnings at the point a credential is completed, beyond what extra years of study would predict. It fits signaling, but completion can also open licensed jobs, finish a key skill, or mark people who differ in unobserved ways. It does not prove that education is pure signaling.

    Optional: from skills to wages
    MPL = Δoutput / Δlabor (physical units)
    MRPL = MR × MPL (revenue); for a price taker, MRPL = P × MPL
    A wage-taking firm hires while MRPL ≥ wage

    MRU's lesson calls a dollar-valued gain the “marginal product of labor”. Standard texts call that the marginal revenue product. Keep the units straight: output versus money.

    Training raises skills→MPL and MRPL rise→Labor demand shifts right→With supply unchanged, wage and employment rise

    That market result needs a broad rise in demand for the skill, not one person's degree. If many people gain the skill, labor supply also shifts, and the wage effect can be smaller.

    10. The Econ Duel: learning or signaling?

    Tyler Cowen and Alex Tabarrok agree that graduates earn more. They disagree about why, and about what that means for society.

    SpeakerPositionExamples used
    CowenLasting learning explains much of the gap; education builds critical thinking and social skills.Educated self-employed workers, Scandinavian schooling changes and Asian development; education works alongside infrastructure and governance.
    TabarrokCredentials reveal ability and persistence and open doors to careers.Students cheering canceled classes yet valuing the degree; a credential race that disadvantages people without degrees.

    These are debate positions. The video does not give enough study detail to split the wage gap into learning and signaling shares.

    Private return versus social return

    Private return

    The student's extra earnings and other benefits, minus tuition, effort, time and forgone pay.

    Social return

    Extra real output, better job matching and spillovers, minus the real resources used. Tuition can be a transfer; teachers' time and buildings are real costs.

    In a pure credential race, one person's degree can lift their rank without raising total skill. If everyone responds by studying longer for unchanged jobs, credential inflation學歷門檻膨脹 uses resources and shuts out non-graduates. Better matching can still create some social value, and some jobs really have become more complex.

    What evidence could separate the explanations?

    ComparisonWhat it can testWhy caution remains
    A credible change in access to schoolingThe effect of extra schooling on those affected.Needs a valid comparison group and no other changes at the same time.
    Credential status with similar measured skillsThe information or eligibility value of a degree.Completers may still differ in unobserved traits.
    Direct tests of job skills and outputWhether learning raises capability.Tests can miss skills; tools and jobs also matter.
    How employer beliefs change over timeWhether early credential-based pay adjusts to performance.Performance is noisy, and early career paths persist.
    Cross-country schooling and incomeA pattern to explain.Institutions, health, capital and reverse causation can drive it.

    A wage gap compares different people, so it lacks the counterfactual反事實: what the same people would earn without the degree. No single comparison settles the debate, and being unable to measure the shares is an evidence limit, not a reason to assume they are equal.

    11. Do not mix these up

    Uncertainty means asymmetric information.

    Both sides can lack the same facts. Look for a relative gap.

    Adverse selection means any bad outcome.

    Hidden types must change who participates.

    Moral hazard means dishonest people.

    It is an incentive and monitoring problem; no fraud is needed.

    The signing date is the whole distinction.

    Ask: selection among types, or behavior under incentives?

    Adverse selection always destroys the market.

    It can leave a smaller, stable pool.

    Screening and signaling are the same.

    Check who starts the revealing action.

    An expensive signal is automatically credible.

    Ask whether a low type would copy it.

    More insured care is all waste.

    Compare each visit's benefit with its resource cost.

    Cost sharing has no downside.

    It weakens protection and can deter valuable care.

    A graduate wage gap proves learning.

    Signaling and ability bias can contribute too.

    12. Cold call practice

    What is adverse selection?

    Hidden characteristics change who participates, so the uninformed side faces a worse average pool, as when a pooled used-car offer drives good cars away.

    Why can risk aversion keep low-risk buyers insured?

    They value avoiding a large uncertain loss, so they may pay a premium above their expected claims.

    What is moral hazard?

    Behavior that others cannot fully observe changes because someone else bears part of the consequences, as when an insured shop cuts fire precautions.

    How do reviews reduce moral hazard?

    They inform buyers and make bad conduct cost future business. Fake reviews weaken both effects.

    Why can reliable test information be underprovided?

    It is costly to produce, but once shared it is nonrival and hard to exclude, so users free ride.

    How does signaling differ from screening?

    The informed side signals, for example with a warranty. The uninformed side screens, for example with an inspection.

    What is cheap talk?

    A costless claim any type can make, so it does not separate types.

    What is the sheepskin effect?

    An earnings jump at credential completion beyond smooth years-of-schooling effects. It fits signaling but does not prove pure signaling.

    When can a contract menu screen types?

    When different risks reliably choose different options. The resulting pool still has to be checked, not assumed.

    13. Answer templates

    Adverse selection

    “One party privately knows its type. Common terms attract a different mix of participants, which changes the quality or risk of the pool. That feedback can worsen the terms and eliminate useful trades, although the market need not collapse completely.”

    Moral hazard

    “The contract changes the private consequences of an action that is hard to observe. Behavior can then shift costs to someone else. I identify the action that changes and compare monitoring or incentive fixes with their costs.”

    Principal-agent

    “The principal delegates to an agent with better information. Their incentives differ, and the principal cannot fully verify the agent's action or advice. A good remedy narrows the information gap or the conflict, rather than simply assuming the expert is dishonest.”

    Credible signaling

    “The informed party takes an action that an inferior type would not want to copy. Buyers then update their beliefs. The key is differential cost and enforceability, not expense alone.”

    Education debate

    “Education can build skills and signal existing traits at the same time, and prior ability can raise both schooling and earnings. A wage premium alone cannot identify the causal shares, and private returns need not equal the gain in social output.”
