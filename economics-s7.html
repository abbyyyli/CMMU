    GDP and Economic Growth

    GDP tells us how much an economy produces. Once prices and population are taken out, it becomes the best single gauge of average living standards. This session measures GDP, compares it across time and countries, and asks why some countries grew rich while others stayed poor.

    1. What GDP counts

    MRU pictures the economy as a giant supermarket. Every time a finished good or service is sold, the register rings up its price. Add up a year of sales and you have GDP.

    Gross Domestic Product國內生產毛額 is the market value of all finished goods and services produced within a country in a year. Each phrase is a counting rule.

    PhraseRuleMRU exampleDo not confuse with
    Market valuePrices let us add apples and haircuts.Polar bears are left out because there is no easy market price, not because they lack value.Total value to society.
    Finished (final)Count goods sold to their end user.Eggs for a home omelet count; eggs a baker uses for a cake do not, because the cake's price already includes them.Intermediate goods中間財, used up inside another product.
    Capital goods資本財Count them: they are finished goods.A tractor sold to a farm counts, even though it helps produce other goods.Intermediate inputs, which are not resold as part of another product.
    Produced in a yearCount new production only.A newly built house counts; selling an old house adds nothing.Resales, which were counted when first produced.
    Within a countryLocation of production decides.French wine bought in the United States adds to France's GDP.GNI, which counts income earned by a country's residents wherever they produce.

    GDP also leaves out purely financial trades, such as buying shares, and transfer payments, such as pensions. Neither one produces anything new.

    The transactions are illustrative Thai examples, not national-accounts data.

    2. Nominal versus real GDP

    GDP can rise because the economy produces more, or only because prices went up. MRU calls the second kind a mirage.

    TermDefinitionExample
    Nominal GDPOutput valued at the prices of the year it is produced.A loaf of bread that cost a dime now costs a couple of dollars.
    Real GDPOutput valued at the same set of base-year prices, so it changes only when quantities change.Valuing this year's rice and haircuts at last year's prices.
    GDP deflator平減指數Nominal GDP divided by real GDP, times 100: a price index for all domestic output.Nominal 8.66 million ÷ real 7.40 million × 100 ≈ 117.
    Real GDP per capitaReal GDP divided by population.Real GDP +5.7% with population +5% leaves about +0.7% per person.
    RecessionA broad fall in real economic activity; falling real GDP is part of the definition.US real GDP fell 3.6% in 2009, and unemployment spiked.

    3. Is GDP per person a good measure of living standards?

    MRU's answer: imperfect, but probably the best single measure, because it moves with many things money cannot buy directly.

    What it tracks

    Countries with higher real GDP per capita tend to have longer life expectancy, happier people on average, higher scores on the UN Human Development Index, and, over time, rising incomes for the poorest 20%.

    What it misses

    Distribution. Nigeria, Pakistan and Honduras have similar real GDP per capita, yet about 80%, 60% and 33% of their people live on under $2 a day. Nigeria's income is far more unequal.

    It also leaves out leisure, unpaid household work and pollution. Because rich countries tend to be healthier, people sometimes read the link as cause and effect. It is a correlation: good institutions could raise both income and health.

    4. Two ways to split GDP

    The same total can be split by who buys the output, or by who receives the income from making it.

    National spending approach: Y = C + I + G + NX, where NX = exports − imports
    Factor income approach: GDI = employee compensation + rent + interest + profit
    PartDefinitionMRU exampleDo not confuse with
    C ConsumptionHousehold spending on final goods and services; about 63% of US GDP.Cakes and massages.Spending on used goods.
    I InvestmentNew capital goods, usually bought by businesses.Computers and tractors.Buying shares or bonds.
    G Government purchasesGoods and services bought directly by government, both everyday items and capital.Paper and pens; tanks and computers.Transfer payments移轉支付 such as a social security check, which buy nothing.
    NX Net exportsExports minus imports; small for the United States.Foreigners buy our goods; we subtract what C, I and G bought from abroad.A judgment that imports are bad.

    Why exclude the pension check? When the retiree spends it, that spending is already counted as consumption, so counting the check too would double count. Why split GDP at all? Consumption, investment and government purchases respond to different forces, and a recession calls for different tools depending on which one fell.

    The income side works because every baht spent on final output ends up with workers, landlords, lenders or entrepreneurs. In theory GDP equals gross domestic income (GDI). They are measured in different ways, so the two numbers differ slightly, and economists watch both in a recession because one may give an earlier signal.

    5. Comparing countries: the haircut test

    To compare GDP across countries, we must account for the fact that the same dollar buys more in a poor country.

    In IndiaPriceWhy
    iPhone 7 PlusAbout 70,000 rupees, roughly $1,000: at least the US price.Tradable可貿易: if it were much cheaper in India, people would buy there and resell in the US. Arbitrage pulls prices together.
    Haircut and head massage50 rupees, under $1.Service: hard to trade. The barber, Raju, is as good as any in the US but earns $10 a day or less, versus maybe $10 an hour in the US. Laws stop him moving, so labor is immobile.

    Services that cannot move, such as haircuts, housecleaning, taxis and massage, are much cheaper in poorer countries. This is the Balassa-Samuelson effect. Because services are cheap, the overall price level is lower and each dollar goes further. Converting a poor country's GDP at market exchange rates therefore understates its living standard. Purchasing power parity購買力平價 (PPP) values output at a common set of prices, which MRU calls the most accurate way we know to compare countries.

    High productivity in factories and offices→High wages across the economy→A barber's outside option rises→Haircut supply shifts left→Higher service prices

    The supply and demand numbers are a teaching model. MRU's video explains the price gap through low wages and immobile labor; the productivity link is the standard Balassa-Samuelson reasoning.

    How to build this graph
    1. Put haircuts per day on the horizontal axis and baht per haircut on the vertical axis. Draw D sloping down and S1 sloping up; mark E1, P1 and Q1.
    2. Ask what changed. A higher wage elsewhere raises the cost of each haircut, so the supply curve shifts left (up) to S2.
    3. Mark E2 where D meets S2. Read P2 above P1 and Q2 below Q1. If local incomes also rise, shift D right too, and P rises further.

    中文邏輯理髮便宜不是因為理髮師比較差，而是整個國家的工資低。可貿易品被套利拉平，服務拉不平，所以要用 PPP 才公平。

    6. How big are the gaps?

    Measured in PPP dollars, the differences between countries are not small. They are often 10, 20, 50, sometimes 100 times.

    IMF data as used by MRU. *MRU states the United States is 90 times the CAR but does not read out the basket count; 90 × 6 baskets gives about 540, roughly $54,000.

    The Central African Republic is small, landlocked and has suffered civil war; MRU calls it probably the poorest country in the world. Mexico is about 29 times richer than the CAR, and the United States about 3 times richer than Mexico. Countries we lump together as "poor" are not in the same league: Mexico is much closer to the United States than to the truly destitute.

    For most of history, average income barely grew. Sustained growth began with the Industrial Revolution and then bent sharply upward, the "hockey stick" of human prosperity. Today's gaps are therefore recent: they reflect when countries started growing and how steadily they kept going.

    7. Growth rates are crucial

    The main reason the United States is rich is simply that it grew steadily for a long time.

    ToolDefinitionExample
    Compound growth複利Each year's growth builds on last year's higher level.At 3% a year, income is about 2.8 times higher after 35 years, not 2.05 times.
    Rule of 7070 法則Years to double ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate in percent; it also works backward.US income went from about $2,000 in 1845 to $4,000 in 1880: 35 years, so about 2% a year.
    Ratio scaleEach tick is a doubling, so a straight line means constant growth.On an ordinary scale, steady growth looks like it is speeding up.

    MRU's counterfactuals: had the US grown at 1% a year since 1800, it would be about as rich today as in 1940, when few people had cars and the average income would now count as poverty. At 3% it would have reached today's living standard in 1917, and real GDP per capita today would be about $893,000. At current rates, the US will not reach that level until 2159.

    Comparing poor countries to past US years is imperfect. Every country today has higher life expectancy than the richest countries had in 1800, thanks to spillovers such as smallpox eradication, antibiotics and better sanitation.

    中文邏輯1% 的差距一年看不出來，一百多年後就是好幾倍。富國的秘密不是哪一年長很快，而是長很久都不停。

    8. Growth miracles and growth disasters

    Are poor countries catching up? Since the Industrial Revolution, regions have diverged: "Divergence, Big Time". Country by country, the story splits.

    JapanArgentina
    1950Poor, similar to Mexico.Similar to many Western European economies; about twice Japan's living standard.
    GrowthDoubled living standards about every 8 years; 70 ÷ 8.2 ≈ 8.5% a year.Periods of negative growth; doubled only once in 65 years, about 1.1% a year.
    2015Over 10 times its 1950 level; about twice as prosperous as Argentina.Stagnated.
    Growth miracle

    A long run of very fast per capita growth: Japan, South Korea, China, and perhaps India now. What took the US 200 years of steady growth can happen in about 40.

    Growth disaster

    Long stagnation or decline: Argentina stalled, while Niger and Chad never had significant growth. That means shorter, less happy lives, not just smaller numbers.

    Catch-up收斂 is possible but not automatic. A poor country can grow fast by adopting technology that already exists and adding capital where it is scarce. A country at the frontier must invent new ideas, which is slower. MRU's condition is "the right factors in place", the subject of the next section.

    10. Institutions: one peninsula, two outcomes

    Tyler Cowen calls the division of Korea an almost perfect natural experiment.

    Institutions are laws and regulations, including property rights, reliable courts and political stability, plus cultural norms of honesty, trust and cooperation. Before division, the two Koreas had the same people, culture, language and history and similar economies; if anything, the north was wealthier. After the Second World War, communism was imposed on the North and the South ended up with a relatively free market. Different institutions created different incentives.

    South KoreaNorth Korea
    RulesEntrepreneurs keep profits from serving consumers; failing firms go bankrupt, so capital moves to better uses.Central planning, price controls, no right to start businesses or keep profits.
    Where effort goesCommercial cooperation built on trust.Influencing politics, since the party allocates capital.
    ResultDeveloped-country living standards; Hyundai cars, K-pop, films and cosmetics sold abroad.Periodic starvation, because prices and property rights did not reward farmers for growing enough food.
    From space at nightBright.Dark, except Pyongyang.

    "Almost perfect" is fair, not exact: the two halves also differed in allies, aid and war damage.

    11. Geography and the natural tariff

    The most prosperous cities usually sit on a major coast or river. Water makes trade cheap.

    Cheap water transport→Lower trade costs→Larger markets→More specialization and innovation→Growth

    This is Adam Smith's argument. MRU's map of GDP per square kilometer shows output piled on US coasts and the Great Lakes rivers, Western Europe, Japan, China's coast and Australia's coast. Even today, landlocked countries are on average poorer. Africa is enormous, big enough to hold most of the US, China, India and much of Europe, yet Europe has two to three times Africa's coastline. Africa is the most landlocked continent and has the most poor countries.

    MRU's conclusionLimit
    Being landlocked is a natural tariff天然關稅, a tax on trade. If natural tariffs hurt growth, tariffs set by governments probably do not help either.Fits the standard trade model, where a tariff raises prices and creates deadweight loss. Exceptions argued for specific cases need evidence.
    Growth is not only about policy; some places have natural blessings.Geography is not destiny: landlocked Switzerland is rich, and rail links can cut transport costs.

    Event chain: landlocked → higher transport cost per shipment → supply of imports shifts left, so import prices rise and quantities fall; exporters receive less → less trade and specialization.

    12. When the numbers themselves are weak

    GDP per capita needs good counts of output and people. The poorest countries often have neither.

    The Central African Republic, the poorest country in MRU's comparison, has no reliable birth and death records or recent census. Researchers led by Les Roberts surveyed households by car, boat, motorcycle and on foot and estimated that nearly 6% of the population died in 2022, mostly from malaria, diarrhea and violence. The UN's estimate was about four times lower, because it projected older survey data forward with models that assume stable conditions. Only the survey rate crossed the emergency threshold of one death per 10,000 people a day, which matters for aid. The CAR government and the UN's chief demographer disputed the study.

    The article's claim

    Official figures in fragile states can badly undercount suffering, and weak data plus low attention keep crises unseen.

    Careful reading

    One disputed survey is evidence of large uncertainty, not a settled number. The economics lesson: model-based estimates fail where conditions change fastest, so GDP comparisons at the bottom are rough.

    13. Do not mix these up

    Anything used to make other goods is intermediate.

    Capital goods such as a tractor are finished goods and count.

    GDP counts what citizens produce anywhere.

    That is GNI. GDP counts production inside the borders.

    Investment means buying stocks.

    In GDP, investment is new capital goods.

    All government spending is G.

    Transfers such as pensions buy nothing and are excluded.

    The US economy grew 55 times since 1950.

    That is nominal. Real is about 8 times; per person about 3.6 times.

    Same GDP per capita means same poverty.

    Distribution differs: Nigeria versus Honduras.

    Haircuts are cheap in India because barbers are worse.

    Wages are low economy-wide and labor cannot move.

    Compare countries at market exchange rates.

    Use PPP, or poor countries look poorer than they are.

    A one-point growth gap is small.

    Over 140 years, 3% versus 2% is about 3.9 times.

    Poor countries automatically catch up.

    Argentina, Niger and Chad did not.

    Rich countries have more capital, so capital explains growth.

    That is the immediate cause; ask why capital accumulated.

    Geography is destiny.

    Natural tariffs matter, but institutions and infrastructure can offset them.

    14. Cold call practice

    What is GDP?

    The market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year, for example cars built in Rayong and massages sold in Bangkok this year.

    Why does a tractor count but a baker's eggs do not?

    The eggs are used up inside the cake, whose price already includes them. The tractor is a finished capital good that is not resold as part of another product.

    Why are transfer payments not part of G?

    They buy no output. When recipients spend the money, it is counted as consumption, so counting the transfer too would double count.

    What is the difference between nominal and real GDP?

    Nominal uses current prices, so it rises with inflation; real holds prices at a base year, so it rises only when output rises.

    Is real GDP per capita a good measure of living standards?

    It is the best single measure because it correlates with life expectancy, happiness and the HDI, but it misses distribution, leisure and the environment.

    What is purchasing power parity?

    Valuing output at a common set of prices so that cross-country comparisons reflect real buying power, since a dollar buys more in poor countries.

    What is the Balassa-Samuelson effect?

    Services that cannot be traded are cheaper in poorer countries because wages there are low, so the price level is lower; a haircut in India costs under $1.

    What is the rule of 70?

    Doubling time is about 70 divided by the growth rate in percent: 2% doubles in about 35 years, 7% in about 10.

    What are MRU's five key institutions for growth?

    Property rights, honest government, political stability, a dependable legal system, and competitive and open markets.

    Why is being landlocked called a natural tariff?

    High transport costs act like a tax on every import and export, shrinking markets and specialization.

    15. Answer templates

    Define GDP

    “GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year. Final means we skip intermediate inputs to avoid double counting, and produced means resales and transfers do not count.”

    Nominal, real, per capita

    “Nominal GDP uses current prices, so it rises with inflation. Real GDP holds prices fixed, so it shows whether we produce more. Dividing by population gives real GDP per capita, a rough measure of average living standards.”

    Comparing countries

    “Tradable goods cost about the same everywhere because of arbitrage, but services cannot be traded and workers cannot move freely. Poor countries have low wages, so services and the price level are lower. PPP adjusts for that.”

    Growth rates

    “By the rule of 70, 2% growth doubles income every 35 years and 7% every 10. Small differences compound: Japan's fast doubling turned half of Argentina's income in 1950 into twice Argentina's by 2015.”

    Why some countries are rich

    “The immediate reason is productive workers with more physical capital, human capital and technology, organized by entrepreneurs. The deeper reason is incentives created by institutions: property rights, honest government, stability, dependable courts and open markets. North and South Korea show how different institutions can produce a miracle and a disaster from the same start.”
