    Inflation and the Quantity Theory of Money

    Prices in general rise when more money chases the same goods. This chapter shows how inflation is measured, why money growth drives it, why unpredictable inflation hurts even when wages keep up, who wins and who loses, and why governments create it anyway.

    1. What runaway inflation looks like: Zimbabwe

    Around 2000, President Robert Mugabe needed money to reward allies and buy off rivals. He had already taxed almost everything, and investors had left. So he printed money.

    Print money to pay for spending→No new factories or productivity, so the same goods→More money chases the same goods→Prices rise→The government needs even more money→Print more

    That loop is why it got out of control. The faster prices rose, the more the government printed, and the faster prices rose again.

    1. ~2000Prices rising about 50% a year
    2. 2001100% a year
    3. 2002200% a year
    4. 2003600% a year
    5. 2006Over 1,000% a year. Z$417 for one sheet of toilet paper, not one roll
    6. 2008Thousands of percent a month, then a peak of 7.6 billion percent a month and Z$100 trillion notes. By the end of the year the Zimbabwe dollar is effectively dead and foreign currencies are made legal

    People spent cash the moment they got it, because money held in the morning bought less by evening. A million Zimbabwe dollars might buy a chicken, if you were lucky. Germany in 1923, China in 1949 and Yugoslavia in 1994 went through the same thing, each time because a government desperate for cash had little to fall back on except the printing press.

    Hyperinflation惡性通膨 has no single official line. A common textbook threshold is 50% or more per month.

    2. Measuring inflation

    Supply and demand push single prices up and down all the time. Think of each price as a ping pong ball bouncing inside an elevator. Inflation is the elevator going up.

    3. The quantity theory of money: M × V = P × Y

    Follow one dollar for a year. Tyler buys a pupusa from Don. Don gives the dollar to his daughter, who spends it on a pony ride. It ends up with Alex, who loses it in his couch, finds it, and buys a coffee on a road trip. One dollar, spent three times.

    LetterWhat it isIn the storyFor a whole economy
    MMoneyThe one dollar billThe money supply貨幣供給: all the money in the economy
    VVelocity流通速度3, since it was spent three timesHow many times the average dollar is spent on final goods and services in a year
    PPrice levelThe prices of the pupusa, pony ride and coffeeThe average price of all final goods and services
    YReal outputThe pupusa, pony ride and coffeeReal GDP
    Left side: M × V

    All the money times how often it is spent. This is what buyers paid in total, which is nominal GDP.

    Right side: P × Y

    Everything sold times its price. This is what sellers received in total, which is also nominal GDP.

    Everything sold is bought by someone, so the two sides are always equal. The equation is an identity: true by definition. On its own it says nothing about cause. The theory comes in the next section, when we add facts about which letters move.

    4. What causes inflation

    Rewrite the identity as P = M × V ÷ Y. If the price level changes, only three things can be behind it.

    Y: real GDP

    A 10% rise in one year would be astonishing growth. A 10% fall would be a great depression. It moves too little.

    V: velocity

    About 7 in the US in recent years. It depends on habits like weekly or biweekly pay. It might drift to 6 or 8, usually not much more.

    M: money

    Can grow very fast, as in Zimbabwe or Peru. The only candidate for large, lasting price rises.

    When more money chases the same amount of goods and services, prices must rise.

    In growth rates, the identity becomes a rule of thumb you can use in an answer:

    Inflation ≈ money growth + velocity growth − real GDP growth
    Peru

    Something costing 1 inti in 1980 cost 10 million intis by 1995. Money and prices rose together. In 1990 money grew about 6,000% a year, and inflation was about 6,000% a year.

    Another MRU video puts Peru's 1990 inflation at about 7,500%. The point is the same either way: money and prices rose together.

    About 110 countries, 1960 to 1990

    Plot inflation against money growth and the points lie close to a line: one extra point of money growth goes with about one extra point of inflation. The US sits near the bottom, Poland higher up, Argentina at about 80%. Indonesia sits in the middle, with inflation below its money growth.

    PrincipleIn plain words
    1. Money is neutral in the long run貨幣中立Double the money supply and, in time, prices double. Real output is not changed.
    2. Inflation is a monetary phenomenonMilton Friedman: inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." Large, lasting inflation always comes with fast money growth.
    3. Central banks control inflationCentral banks largely control the money supply, so they largely control inflation.

    Reading the Thai charts together

    The slides also show Thailand's narrow money supply, M1, from the Bank of Thailand. It rose from about 1.9 trillion baht in 2017 to about 3.6 trillion in 2026, so it nearly doubled. That is about 7% a year. Over the same years Thai inflation stayed low. The growth rule explains the gap. Real output grew, which absorbed part of the new money. Velocity fell, because people held more money relative to what they spent. So 7% money growth did not turn into 7% inflation.

    M1 values are read off the chart. The explanation is our own use of the quantity theory, not from the slides.

    5. Why unpredictable inflation is costly

    Inflation raises wages along with prices. If everyone knew it would be 2% or 8% every year, people could plan for it. The trouble is that nobody knows.

    PlaceHow inflation moved
    United States1.3% in 1964, 5.9% in 1970, 11% in 1974, 14% in 1980, 3% in 1983. People were caught off guard each time.
    Peru77% in 1986, then thousands of percent in 1990, then 73% in 1992.
    Price confusion價格混淆

    A price is a signal wrapped in an incentive. When oil gets dearer, users should economize. With inflation every price is rising, so nobody can tell whether oil is scarcer or there is simply more money around. Prices become noisy, and the economy coordinates less well.

    Money illusion貨幣幻覺

    Mistaking a change in a nominal price for a change in a real price. A movie ticket doubles while wages double too, yet movies "feel" too expensive and people go less.

    A nominal price is the number on the tag. A real price is what it costs in hours of work or other goods. A bowl of noodles going from 30 to 60 baht is no dearer in real terms if wages also doubled.

    6. Real interest rates and arbitrary redistribution

    Loans are written in money. Inflation changes what that money is worth by the time it is repaid.

    Real interest rate = nominal interest rate − inflation rate
    A bank lends $100 at 10%. Inflation is 10%. It gets back $110, which buys what $100 bought. Real return: 0.

    The slides show a 1978 mortgage: a 12% nominal rate is a 2% real rate plus 10% inflation. Whether that deal turns out well depends on what inflation actually does next.

    SurpriseBorrowersLendersMRU example
    Inflation higher than expectedGainLoseMortgages from the 1960s were repaid in the cheap dollars of the 1970s.
    Inflation lower than expectedLoseGainA mortgage at 18% around 1981. Inflation fell from about 14% to under 5%, so the real rate became 13%.

    Lenders protect themselves against the inflation they expect. If they expect 10% inflation and want a 5% real return, they charge 15%. Nominal rates rise with expected inflation: the Fisher effect費雪效果. The US chart in the slides shows it. Interest rates followed inflation up in the 1970s, peaked near 20% when inflation hit about 14%, and came down with it.

    Now make inflation high and volatile. Lenders fear a surprise rise. Borrowers fear a surprise fall. Both avoid long contracts, so 20-year mortgages and long business loans become costly and rare. Moving savings to borrowers, which is financial intermediation, starts to break down, and the economy builds less wealth.

    中文邏輯費雪效果只保護「預期到的」通膨。財富重分配來自沒預期到的那一段：通膨比預期高，借款人賺；比預期低，放款人賺。

    7. Expected inflation still hurts savers

    The Fisher effect does not fix one problem: the government taxes nominal interest, including the part that only makes up for inflation.

    CaseNominal rateInflationNominal after 33% taxReal after tax
    Low inflation6%3%4%+1%
    Moderate inflation12%9%8%−1%
    High inflation90%87%60%−27% (shortcut)

    In every case the lender expected inflation correctly and set a 3% real rate before tax. Saving still loses money once inflation is moderate. Cash under the mattress does even worse, since it earns nothing. When inflation is very high, people spend money as fast as they get it. That raises V, and by the quantity theory prices rise faster still.

    The table uses MRU's shortcut: nominal after tax minus inflation. It is close for small rates. At 87% inflation the exact loss is about −14%. Either way the saver loses.

    Optional: why the shortcut breaks at high inflation

    "Nominal after tax minus inflation" is a shortcut that only works for small rates. The exact real return is (1 + nominal after tax) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. MRU leaves 900% inflation as a practice question. Keeping a 3% real rate before tax, the nominal rate is 903%, so the shortcut gives 602% − 900% = −298%. Nobody can lose more than everything they saved. The exact answer is about −30%. Either way, savers lose.

    8. Winners and losers through the ages

    MRU's time machine puts you in three moments in history. The question each time: will inflation, or deflation, make you a winner or a loser?

    WhoThe choiceRight callWhat happened
    US farmer, 1893Inflation has been 0% for two years. Borrow $100 for a tractor?NoDeflation hit (−1.1% in 1893). By 1895 the $100 owed was worth $108 in real terms, on top of interest.
    US retiree, 1946Living on a $25 monthly Social Security check. Lobby for automatic inflation adjustment?YesInflation reached 14% in 1947. By the end of 1948 the check bought what $18.70 used to. Automatic adjustment came in 1975.
    Venezuelan saver, 2013Holding 1,000 bolivars after inflation of 21% in 2012. Pay high fees to move into another currency?YesPrices rose about 40% in a year, so the 1,000 bolivars bought what 712 used to. By the end of 2018 they were worth 0.11 in real terms.

    The pattern: anyone holding a fixed amount of money, as savings, a loan they made or a fixed income, is hurt by surprise inflation. Anyone owing a fixed amount is hurt by surprise deflation. Indexation物價連動, which ties a payment to the price level, protects against both.

    9. Why governments create inflation

    If inflation is this costly, why does any government create it? MRU gives two reasons and one warning.

    Reason 1: an inflation tax通膨稅

    Printing money and spending it moves wealth from everyone holding money to the government. It is a poor tax, used when a government cannot raise money any other way, as in Zimbabwe.

    Reason 2: a short-run boost

    An unexpected increase in money can raise real output for a while. That can help in a recession. It can also be abused, for example to win an election.

    The parable of inflation

    1. A government pays its soldiers with newly printed money. They buy bread. The baker thinks demand is up, works longer, hires help, bakes more and raises his price.
    2. The tailor and the carpenter feel the same, because soldiers buy from them too.
    3. The baker goes to buy shirts and finds their price has risen. All three earned more dollars, but those dollars buy less. Their real wages fell.
    4. Next time the government must print more to buy the same goods. Everyone rushes to spend before prices rise, so V goes up and prices rise faster.
    5. They catch on. They expect inflation, stop working extra hours, and the boost is gone.

    The warning: stopping inflation is costly. Cutting money growth works the same story in reverse. Output falls until prices adjust, which is a recession. MRU compares inflation to a drug: it lifts you at first, then you need more for the same effect, then you need it just to feel normal, and quitting brings withdrawal. The US lived this. By the late 1970s inflation no longer lowered unemployment, and the country had stagflation停滯性通膨: high inflation and high unemployment together. Inflation came down in the early 1980s, under Ronald Reagan, at the price of a severe recession in 1981 and 1982.

    中文邏輯短期印錢會讓大家「以為」生意變好；等發現什麼都漲了，就只剩物價上漲。要把通膨降回來，得先挨一段衰退。

    10. India 2016: when 86% of the cash disappeared

    An MRU "Economists in the Wild" video. Zimbabwe printed too much money. India did the opposite, suddenly and on purpose.

    November 2016: target "black money"→Large notes can no longer be spent, only deposited→86% of the currency gone overnight→About 8 months to print enough new notes

    Black money meant cash from ill-gotten sources, such as a corrupt official's hoard of bribes or counterfeit notes used to fund terrorism. People could deposit old notes at a bank if they could show the money was clean. Predictions differed widely. The government did not expect much harm. Some private economists predicted a recession of 7% or 8% of the economy.

    A natural experiment自然實驗

    Some districts got new notes quickly, so their cash shortage was short. Others waited much longer. Comparing them shows the effect of losing cash.

    Measuring it without good data

    Regional data in India are weak, so the researchers used an employment survey and satellite images of night lights, down to about one square kilometer. Human-made light tracks economic activity.

    The published study found that districts hit harder lost more jobs and night-time light, had slower bank lending and moved faster to e-wallets and cards. Nationally, activity fell about 3 percentage points in December 2016, and the effects faded over the following months. More than 99% of the old notes eventually came back into banks.

    In the language of this chapter, usable M fell suddenly. Output fell in the short run before cash returned, which fits section 9: money is not neutral in the short run. It does not contradict long-run neutrality, since the effects faded.

    Findings: Chodorow-Reich, Gopinath, Mishra and Narayanan, "Cash and the Economy: Evidence from India's Demonetization."

    11. Do not mix these up

    Prices doubled since 1984, so people are poorer.

    Compare real values. Wages rose more than prices on average.

    M × V = P × Y proves money causes inflation.

    The identity proves nothing by itself. The theory adds that V and Y are fairly stable.

    Money is never neutral.

    It is neutral in the long run. In the short run an unexpected change can move output.

    Inflation is costly because prices go up.

    Wages go up too. The costs come from unpredictability, transfers and broken long-term lending.

    Inflation always hurts borrowers.

    Unexpected inflation helps borrowers. Unexpected deflation hurts them.

    If inflation is fully expected, savers are fine.

    Not when tax falls on nominal interest: 12% nominal with 9% inflation gives −1% real after tax.

    Disinflation means prices are falling.

    Disinflation means inflation is slowing. Prices still rise. Deflation is when they fall.

    India's demonetization was too much money, like Zimbabwe.

    It was a sudden cut in usable cash, the reverse case.

    12. Cold call practice

    What is inflation? Give an example.

    Inflation is a rise in the average level of prices. If the CPI goes from 100 to 103 in a year, inflation is 3%. One good getting dearer is not inflation by itself.

    What is the CPI, and why is it weighted?

    The average price of a basket of goods and services that consumers buy. Items count by their share of spending, so housing moves it more than toothbrushes.

    A price index rises from 120 to 126. What is the inflation rate?

    (126 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 5%.

    What are M, V, P and Y?

    The money supply, the velocity of money, the price level and real GDP. M × V is total spending and P × Y is total sales, so both equal nominal GDP.

    What causes large, lasting inflation?

    Fast growth in the money supply. Real GDP and velocity move too little to explain it. Zimbabwe printed money to pay for spending and prices rose billions of percent a month.

    Money grows 8% a year, velocity is steady and real GDP grows 3%. Roughly what is inflation?

    About 5%: 8 + 0 − 3.

    What does "money is neutral in the long run" mean?

    A change in the money supply eventually changes prices only, not real output. Double the money and prices double.

    What is price confusion?

    When all prices are rising, people cannot tell whether a price rose because a good is scarcer or because of inflation, so price signals become noisy.

    What is money illusion?

    Mistaking a nominal price change for a real one, such as feeling movies are too expensive after ticket prices and wages both doubled.

    What is the real interest rate on a 12% mortgage with 10% inflation?

    2%: the nominal rate minus inflation, as in the 1978 example from the slides.

    Who gains from unexpected inflation?

    Borrowers, because they repay with money worth less. Lenders, cash savers and people on fixed incomes lose.

    What is the Fisher effect?

    Nominal interest rates rise with expected inflation. Expecting 10% inflation and wanting a 5% real return, a lender charges 15%.

    Why does volatile inflation reduce long-term lending?

    Lenders fear a surprise rise and borrowers fear a surprise fall, so both avoid long contracts and financial intermediation breaks down.

    Why can expected inflation still hurt savers?

    Tax is charged on nominal interest, including the part that only offsets inflation. At 12% nominal, 9% inflation and a 33% tax, the real return is −1%.

    Why do governments create inflation?

    As a tax when they cannot raise money any other way, and for a short-run boost to output. The boost fades once people expect inflation.

    Why is stopping inflation costly?

    Cutting money growth lowers output before prices adjust, causing a recession, as in the US in 1981 and 1982.

    What is stagflation?

    High inflation and high unemployment at the same time, as in the US in the late 1970s.

    Why is India's demonetization called a natural experiment?

    Districts got new notes at different speeds by chance, so researchers could compare places with short and long cash shortages.

    13. Answer templates

    Define and give an example

    "Inflation is a rise in the average price level, measured by the percentage change in a price index such as the CPI. If the CPI goes from 100 to 103, inflation is 3%. One price rising on its own is a relative price change."

    What causes inflation

    "By the quantity theory, M times V equals P times Y. Real output and velocity change slowly, so large and lasting inflation comes from growth in the money supply. Zimbabwe printed money to pay for spending, and prices rose billions of percent a month."

    Why inflation is costly

    "Wages rise with prices, so the main problem is unpredictability. High, volatile inflation blurs price signals, moves wealth between borrowers and lenders, and makes long-term lending too risky."

    Who wins and who loses

    "When inflation is higher than expected, borrowers gain because they repay in cheaper money. Lenders, cash savers and people on fixed incomes lose. When inflation is lower than expected, it is the reverse."

    Why governments do it

    "Governments use inflation as a tax when they cannot raise money any other way, or to boost output in the short run. The boost fades once people expect inflation, and bringing inflation down again usually causes a recession."
