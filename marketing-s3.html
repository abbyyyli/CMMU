    Strategic Marketing Management · Session 3

    Marketing Research

    Research reduces uncertainty; it does not eliminate it. Start from the management decision, choose the method the question needs, study the right people, and never claim more than the evidence supports.

    Chapter map

    Business problem→Research question→Objective→Information gap→Method→Sample→Measurement→Analysis and limits→Decision

    1. Why research?

    Managers decide under uncertainty 不確定性 about wants, needs, motivations, drivers and barriers. Research improves the decision; it cannot guarantee it.

    TypeMain questionExample
    DescriptiveWhat is happening?Who buys? How often? How much?
    DiagnosticWhat may explain the problem?Why is satisfaction falling?
    PredictiveWhat might happen if...?What if we launch, change price or run a promotion?
    Business research

    A real management problem, practical, fast and usable.

    Academic research

    Explanation, theory, prior studies and explicit hypotheses. A different purpose, not a higher quality.

    2. Question before method

    The professor's rule: you must have a question, then define objectives. The method comes last.

    Wrong order

    "Let's run a survey." Then invent a question for it.

    Right order

    Question → objective → information needed → method.

    Start with the decision the manager must make. Weak drink sales might call for a price change, clearer communication, wider distribution or an improved experience. Each choice requires different evidence. A survey asking whether people like the product cannot reliably choose among those actions unless it measures the relevant uncertainty.

    Business problem versus research question

    "Sales of a new drink are weak" is a symptom 症狀. Research questions turn it into something evidence can answer:

    • Are customers aware of the product?
    • Do target customers understand the benefit?
    • Is the price seen as too high?
    • Why did trial customers not buy again?

    A useful research objective

    WeakUnderstand consumers.
    BetterUnderstand the main barriers preventing Bangkok office workers from trying Product X.
    BestIdentify and rank the main barriers preventing Bangkok office workers aged 25 to 40 from trying Product X, so management can decide whether to change price, communication or distribution.

    A good objective names who is studied, what must be learned, and which decision it supports. If the objective is vague, the whole design becomes vague.

    When not to do research

    No time or money; cost above expected value; the result will not change the decision; the decision is already made; the information already exists; timing makes it irrelevant; or there is no clear question.

    3. Research process and evidence sources

    Problem identification→Research design→Method→Sampling→Data collection→Analysis→Report→Follow-up

    Three parties are involved: the company writes a research brief, the research firm proposes and runs the study, and respondents provide data. A vague brief can produce research that is technically correct but useless to management.

    Secondary data 次級資料

    Already exists: government reports, company records, industry reports, published statistics. Check it first.

    Primary data 初級資料

    Collected for this question: surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, experiments. Collect it only for the remaining gap; it costs time and money.

    4. Qualitative versus quantitative

    Qualitative is for understanding; quantitative is for measurement.

    Qualitative 質性Quantitative 量化
    QuestionsWhy? How?Who? How many? How often? How much?
    Good forMotivations, barriers, emotions, meanings, decision processes, concept and ad developmentAwareness, usage, satisfaction, purchase intention, frequency, group differences
    MethodsFocus groups, in-depth interviewsStructured surveys
    Evidence standardSaturation: new interviews add no new themesThe sample can represent the population
    Not forCounting or population percentagesFully explaining why

    Why understanding comes before counting in an unfamiliar problem. EV interviews might reveal charging access, price, battery concerns and resale uncertainty in customers' own words. Those themes help design a survey that measures their prevalence and importance among realistic buyers. Ten interviews do not establish that a barrier affects a particular percentage of Thailand. Conversely, a large survey can precisely count the wrong options if the researcher omitted an important barrier.

    5. Sampling

    The first sampling question is not "how many?" but "who is the right respondent?"

    ConceptWhat it establishesLimit
    Target population and screenDefine whose experience or decision matters. A recent Siam Paragon visit is relevant to a study of its recent service.A valid screen does not make recruitment representative.
    Probability samplingKnown selection probabilities support population estimates and sampling-error calculations under the design.Coverage, nonresponse and measurement errors can remain.
    Non-probability samplingConvenient or purposive recruitment can explore relevant experiences.Selection probabilities are unknown; a large count alone cannot justify population percentages.
    SaturationFurther qualitative discussions add no materially new themes for the question.Theme coverage differs from representing population proportions.

    "We interviewed 100 people, so the results represent Thailand" is wrong. Sample size is not representativeness.

    6. From concept to measurement

    Conceptual framework (class example)

    PackagingFunctional benefitsLifestyleHealth benefits
    →
    Intention to buy
    Independent variables 自變數Dependent variable 依變數

    The arrows show relationships we want to investigate. They do not prove cause.

    Conceptual versus operational definition

    Abstract conceptHealth benefits
    ↓
    Conceptual definition: what it meansLow fat, low calories, high fiber, nutritional value, no sugar
    ↓
    Operational definition: how we measure it"I prefer my ice cream to be low fat." "I prefer ice cream with low calories." Rated on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree).

    The health-benefit example shows a construct becoming observable. First define which aspects count as health benefits; then write items that reflect those aspects; finally specify a response scale and how responses will be interpreted. The class uses 1 = strongly disagree through 4 = strongly agree. Those numbers code answers. They do not prove that respondents understand the wording, that the items cover the intended construct, or that stated preference predicts buying.

    注意問卷有數字不代表量對了。用字、概念定義、受訪者理解和偏誤都會影響品質。

    Validity versus reliability

    Validity 效度: are we measuring what we intend to? Reliability 信度: is the measurement consistent? A survey can be consistently wrong.

    Reliable and validConsistent and on target
    Reliable, not validConsistently measures the wrong thing
    Valid on average, not reliableScattered around the target
    NeitherScattered and off target

    7. Can we trust what people say?

    What consumers say may not fully explain what they do.

    DangerWhat happens
    RationalizationA logical reason given after the fact, when habit, emotion or impulse drove the purchase
    Social desirabilityAnswers that look responsible or healthy: people overstate exercise and recycling
    Courtesy biasOverly positive feedback to be polite, common face to face and where criticism is uncomfortable
    Attitude-behavior gap"I care about sustainability," then buys the cheapest option

    These problems differ. Someone may sincerely explain a purchase using a reason reconstructed afterward (rationalization), report healthier habits to look responsible (social desirability), or praise a service to avoid offending the interviewer (courtesy). An attitude–behavior gap can occur even with an honestly reported attitude: budget or convenience may dominate at checkout. Neutral wording and additional behavioral evidence improve interpretation, but do not make motives directly observable.

    Garbage in, garbage out: statistics cannot rescue a bad question. When possible, combine stated attitudes with observed behavior or transaction data.

    8. Survey, observation and experiment

    Survey

    Door-to-door, mall intercept, phone, online, mobile, paper. No single best mode: choose by respondent, complexity, cost, speed and control.

    Observation

    Records what people actually do and avoids some self-report bias, but cannot directly show motives. Mystery shoppers check real service, prices and displays.

    Experiment

    Change a treatment (X) and observe the outcome (O). Experiments are designed to test effects; credible control conditions are needed to isolate a cause.

    Choose the mode for the information needed

    Method / settingUseful taskTrade-off
    In-depth interview / focus groupExplore an unfamiliar buying process or an early storyboard.Rich meaning; not a population vote.
    Online / phone surveyMeasure defined attributes among reachable qualified respondents.Reach, question complexity and participation differ by mode.
    Mall intercept / central location testEvaluate a product or concept in a controlled setting.Accessible for testing; recruited visitors may not reflect all buyers.
    Observation / mystery shopperSee laundry routines, navigation, displays or actual service execution.Shows actions; one visit does not represent all experiences or explain motives.
    Controlled taste test / experimentCompare formulas or test a defined change.Control helps isolate a difference; realism and alternative explanations still matter.

    Correlation is not causation: the POP display

    Read O → X → O: observe sales, install the point-of-purchase (POP) display, observe sales again. A worked example rises from THB 100,000 to THB 130,000, a 30% before–after increase. That describes the change. It does not isolate the display's contribution because payday, traffic, stockouts or other promotions may also change. The comparison-store tool below asks how much of the increase would plausibly have happened without the display.

    A spurious factor 干擾因素 such as payday, weekend traffic, a competitor stockout, seasonality or another promotion can explain part of a before/after change. Stronger designs use comparison stores or periods and repeated observations.

    Laboratory

    High control, but artificial.

    Field

    Realistic, but less control over other factors.

    A comparable group needs a credible design. Loyalty members may already be more frequent buyers than nonmembers. A stronger test assigns comparable customers or stores to treatment and control when feasible, tracks both over the same period, and checks other changes. A raw member–nonmember difference is not automatically the program's effect. Experiments are designed to test causes; a poorly controlled experiment can still mislead.

    This develops Session 2's counterfactual: estimate the difference a program makes using comparable treatment and control conditions, not self-selected membership alone.

    9. Advanced tools and analysis

    ToolWhat it doesWatch out
    Cluster analysis 集群分析Groups respondents with similar patternsA statistical group may have no clear meaning or action
    Conjoint analysis 聯合分析Asks people to choose between bundles of attributes, revealing trade-offsResults depend on which attributes are tested
    Online communitiesOngoing, low-cost qualitative input with photos and tasksParticipants may not represent the market
    Agile researchFaster, cheaper feedbackSpeed helps only if the company can act on it

    Customer experience management runs Listen → Learn → React. Feedback that arrives weeks late may explain a problem but miss the chance to recover that customer. Proactive research looks for opportunities and threats early; reactive research responds after something goes wrong.

    "63% satisfied" is a result, not an analysis. Ask which group is lower, which attribute matters most, what changed, what explains it, and what evidence is still missing.

    From an output to a management choice

    Cluster analysis may group shoppers by similar habits, but managers still need to interpret the groups and decide whether an offer or channel can serve them. Conjoint analysis compares bundles: for example, a lower price with shorter battery life versus a higher price with longer battery life. It reveals trade-offs that asking for the best of every feature misses. Neither tool can compensate for irrelevant attributes or the wrong respondents.

    A restaurant reporting 63% satisfaction has produced a result. Analysis asks which customers and attributes are weaker, what changed, and which explanations fit. A useful sequence is: measure the problem, explore reasons, observe peak service, test a process change, then measure again. Proactive research spots emerging needs before failure; reactive research diagnoses a problem already observed. Both need an owner who can act on the findings.

    10. Do not mix these up

    A business problem is a research question.

    The problem is a symptom; the question targets one uncertainty.

    We should run a survey.

    The method follows the objective.

    Primary data is always better.

    Existing data may already answer the question at lower cost.

    A big sample is representative.

    Who you sample matters more than how many.

    Saturation means representation.

    Saturation is about themes, not population inference.

    An independent variable is a proven cause.

    It is a proposed explanation to test.

    Reliable means valid.

    A measure can be consistently wrong.

    Sales rose after the display, so it worked.

    Rule out outside factors with a comparison group.

    Observation shows why people act.

    It shows what they do, not their motives.

    Reporting numbers is analysis.

    Analysis explains and links to a decision.

    11. Cold call practice

    Why conduct marketing research?

    To understand consumers, explore opportunities, diagnose problems and measure outcomes, reducing decision risk without eliminating it.

    Why must the question come before the method?

    The objective and the information needed decide the method. Choosing a survey first reverses the logic.

    When should we not do research?

    When the decision is already made, the information exists, time is too short, or the cost exceeds the value.

    Qualitative or quantitative: which is better?

    Neither. Qualitative helps us understand; quantitative helps us measure. They often work in sequence.

    What is saturation?

    The point where more qualitative interviews add no materially new themes.

    Why use a screening question?

    To make sure respondents belong to the target population, for example recent visitors to Siam Paragon.

    What is a conceptual framework?

    A set of proposed relationships between independent variables and an outcome. The arrows are what we test, not proven cause.

    Conceptual versus operational definition?

    The conceptual definition says what a concept means; the operational definition says how we will measure it.

    Why can't we just ask "Why did you buy this?"

    People may not know or report their real motives: rationalization, memory, social desirability and courtesy bias distort answers.

    Why doesn't a before/after sales rise prove the display worked?

    Payday, weekend traffic, a competitor stockout or another promotion could explain it. A comparison group is needed.

    Validity versus reliability?

    Validity is measuring the right thing; reliability is measuring consistently. A measure can be reliable but invalid.

    Cluster versus conjoint analysis?

    Cluster groups similar respondents; conjoint reveals trade-offs between bundles of attributes.

    Example · Choose an EV research method

    The objective is to understand why realistic prospective buyers hesitate to choose an EV. I would begin with in-depth interviews to explore the decision process and unexpected barriers. I would then use a suitably sampled survey to estimate which barriers are most common. Interview themes do not establish population percentages, and purchase intention does not prove actual adoption. A proposed solution would need its own behavioral test.
